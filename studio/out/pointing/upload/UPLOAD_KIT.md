## 3. Description (paste as is; fill the one link)

> Dramatized cartoon. The tourist, the boy and the suitcase are made up. The jars are our picture of the numbers.
> The habit is real: Japanese train drivers point at signals and gauges and call out what they see. The real calls
> are in Japanese; the English ones here are ours.
>
> The number is real too. In a 1994 test by Japan's Railway Technical Research Institute, people pressed the wrong
> button 2.38% of the time when they only looked, and 0.38% of the time when they pointed and said it out loud.
> That is about one mistake for every six. The test had four set-ups: only looking 2.38%, only saying it 1.0%, only
> pointing 0.75%, pointing and saying it 0.38%. The cartoon shows the first and the last.
>
> Sources:
> Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, safety glossary, "pointing and calling" (the 1994 test):
> https://anzeninfo.mhlw.go.jp/yougo/yougo72_1.html
> Wikipedia, "Pointing and calling": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointing_and_calling
> nippon.com, "Pointing and Calling": https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/g02575/
> Mental Floss, on what New York subway conductors point at (see the pinned comment):
> https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/79767/what-are-subway-conductors-always-pointing
> ja.wikipedia, E233 series: a 10-car Tokyo commuter train is rated for 1,582 people:
> https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/JR東日本E233系電車
>
> More on looking without seeing: [PASTE the link to our clown Short, "Count the Phones. Did You See the Clown?"]
>
> #japan

Fact check against the film (SCRIPT_v3 section 6): "For every 6 mistakes people made just looking... about 1" =
