# Seven short scripts (spoken words only)

Each one is a cartoon Short of about a minute with one narrator. Other voices are shown in square brackets. The
spoken length is given above each script.

---

## P

Spoken length: 65 s

He sawed gaps into a railroad every 39 feet.
You see, two companies are racing to build a railroad along a canyon so deep you can't see the bottom.
And whoever finishes first gets the whole country's business.
He's the youngest engineer on the job, and when his boss sees a finger-wide gap between every single rail, his face goes purple.
[boss: "You're wrecking my railroad!"]
But he won't close a single one.
The other company lays their rails end to end, perfectly smooth, and finishes first.
Their trains glide across the canyon without a sound, while his go clickety-clack over every gap.
The newspapers start calling it the clickety-clack line, and everyone switches to the quiet trains.
Then summer comes, and something weird happens.
His gaps start shrinking.
In May, you can fit a coin in them.
By July, barely a sheet of paper, and his boss is thrilled.
[boss: "See? Even your gaps know you were wrong."]
Meanwhile, on the perfect railroad, the rails have started groaning in the afternoon sun, pushing against each other like they want to get out.
On the hottest day anyone can remember, his gaps finally close.
Rail touching rail.
And halfway across the canyon, the perfect railroad kicks sideways like a snake, right as a train rolls onto it.

## Q

Spoken length: 68 s

Japan's railway lab flashed a hundred lights at people, just to catch them on one red.
Try it.
Tap your foot on every green light.
Go. Go. Go. Go. Go. Go. Go! [the light is red]
Did you tap?
She did.
Twelve people just watch, but five press anyway.
Twelve others have to point at the light, and say it.
Not one presses.
You see, after a hundred times, your hand stops waiting for your brain.
That's how the cereal ends up in the fridge.
Pointing and saying it makes your hand wait.
So Japan's train drivers point at their signals, and shout.
[driver shouts: "Signal clear!"]
But the shouting didn't start as science.
The story goes, a steam-train driver's eyes had gone bad.
[steam-train driver shouts: "Signal clear!"]
So he shouted every signal to his coal man.
[coal man shouts: "Clear!"]
His boss rode along, loved it, and made it a rule.
He never knew about the eyes.
New York copied the pointing: conductors must point at this board.
So two New Yorkers held up a sign: "Point here if you are dead sexy."
And the conductors had to point.
Now try again.
This time, point, and say it.

## R

Spoken length: 74 s

Quick test.
Count the people on a phone.
[about 7 seconds with no words: a busy square, people walking past]
Don't lose count.
How many?
Five.
And the clown?
You saw him, right?
In a real experiment, most people on a phone call never saw him.
Psychologist Ira Hyman needed a distraction nobody could miss.
Then a student told him:
[student: "I own a unicycle."]
[student: "...and a clown suit."]
So for one hour, the clown rode circles in the middle of campus.
[interviewer: "Did you see anything unusual?"]
[a friend: "The clown? He was amazing!"]
[a man with headphones: "Dude, the unicycle guy!"]
[a man on a phone: "Sorry... what clown?"]
Even asked straight out, only 1 in 4 people on a call had seen him.
Many spun around to look.
Here's the weird part: people listening to music noticed him.
You see, a call pulls your mind toward someone who isn't there.
So Hyman tried something sneakier.
He hung dollar bills on a branch, right at head height.
Nearly everyone ducked under it.
On a phone?
Only 6 in 100 stopped for the money.
Their bodies dodged it.
Their minds never saw it.
You counted five phones.
You saw the clown.
Did you see the money?

## T

Spoken length: 59 s

If you plant a tree beside an empty road, cars will steer right into it.
You see, the road runs dead straight for miles.
And the only thing beside it is this one tree.
But somehow, somebody hits it every week.
So the town nails a giant flashing arrow to it so nobody can miss it.
Yet the crashes double.
The tow truck guy starts parking out there with a lawn chair.
And he's never made more money in his life.
Eventually, the sheriff drives out to see what's going on.
He goes 15 miles an hour with both hands on the wheel and slams right into it.
He climbs out furious, grabs an ax off the tow truck, and chops the thing down, while the tow truck guy watches his whole business fall over.
The second his cruiser's fixed, the sheriff drives back out to enjoy his nice empty road and plows straight into the stump.
So how does this keep happening?
Well, it's called target fixation.
After miles of nothing, that tree sticks out like a lighthouse.
Especially with a giant flashing arrow pointing at it.
So every driver's eyes lock onto it and won't let go.
And while they stare, their hands slowly turn the wheel toward it.
Even when it's just a stump.

## U

Spoken length: 83 s

She'd never stolen anything.
Not even a paperclip.
Then she read one sign... and wanted to steal.
Arizona's Petrified Forest.
Visitors kept pocketing little pieces.
So the park put up a warning.
It said... "Warning! 14 tons stolen every year... one small piece at a time."
She read it, leaned over to her fiancé, and whispered:
[the woman, whispering: "We'd better get ours now."]
But why would a warning make an honest person want to steal?
Her fiancé's professor, psychologist Robert Cialdini, tested it.
His team hid marked wood along the trails, and kept swapping the signs.
One sign said: "Please don't take the wood."
The other said: "Many visitors take the wood."
With "please don't," only 2% of the pieces vanished.
With "many visitors"... almost 8%.
You see, we don't copy rules.
We copy each other.
"Many people steal" doesn't sound like a warning.
It sounds like what everyone does.
Like a teacher saying, "Half this class cheated."
The other half hears: everybody cheats.
The sign didn't stop thieves.
It gave them permission.
And the bait wood?
It came from the park's "conscience pile": pieces people stole, then mailed back with apology letters.
So some of it got stolen twice.
