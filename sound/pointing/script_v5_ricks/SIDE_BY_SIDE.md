# The ten-sentence test (BENCHMARK_story.md section 9, rule 1)

Each story written as about ten plain sentences, in its own words (ours from the scripts; anti-theft from its final
voiced words; the two Ricks Clips Shorts from their transcripts, joined into ten sentences, not reworded). Read them
one after the other in your head, at speaking pace. Ricks Clips quotes are for private study only.

## 1. Ours, v4 (locked text after today's cut, about 71 s on median takes)

1. Tap your foot on every green light, only green: go, go, go, go, go, go... go! (the last one is red)
2. Did you tap? She did.
3. In 1994, a railway lab in Japan set a trap like this. A hundred lights. Then, red.
4. Twelve people just watched. Five pressed anyway.
5. Twelve others got one extra rule: point at the light and say it. Not one pressed.
6. After a hundred times, your hand stops waiting for your brain. That's how the cereal ends up in the fridge.
7. Pointing and saying it makes your hand wait, which is why Japan's train drivers point at their signals, and shout: "Signal clear!"
8. And where did it come from? The story goes, a steam-train driver's eyes had gone bad, so he shouted every signal to his coal man, who shouted back.
9. His boss rode along, loved it, and made it a rule. He never knew about the eyes.
10. New York copied the pointing, so two New Yorkers held up a sign under the board: "Point here if you are dead sexy." And the conductors had to point. Now try again. This time, point, and say it.

## 2. Ours, v5 proposal (about 67 s on median takes)

1. Tap your foot on every green light, only green: go, go, go, go, go, go... go! (the last one is red)
2. Did you tap? She did.
3. Japan's railway lab set a trap like this. A hundred lights, then red.
4. Twelve people just watch, but five press anyway. Twelve others have to point at the light, and say it. Not one presses.
5. You see, after a hundred times, your hand stops waiting for your brain. That's how the cereal ends up in the fridge.
6. Pointing and saying it makes your hand wait. So Japan's train drivers point at their signals, and shout: "Signal clear!"
7. But the shouting didn't start as science. The story goes, a steam-train driver's eyes had gone bad.
8. So he shouted every signal to his coal man ("Clear!"). His boss rode along, loved it, and made it a rule.
9. He never knew about the eyes.
10. New York copied the pointing: conductors must point at this board. So two New Yorkers held up a sign: "Point here if you are dead sexy." And the conductors had to point. Now try again. This time, point, and say it.

## 3. Ours, anti-theft (our best film: 24.6k views, 91.5 % average viewed; final voiced words, 83 s)

1. She'd never stolen anything. Not even a paperclip. Then she read one sign... and wanted to steal.
2. Arizona's Petrified Forest. Visitors kept pocketing little pieces, so the park put up a warning: "14 tons stolen every year... one small piece at a time."
3. She read it, leaned over to her fiancé, and whispered: "We'd better get ours now."
4. But why would a warning make an honest person want to steal?
5. Her fiancé's professor, psychologist Robert Cialdini, tested it: his team hid marked wood along the trails, and kept swapping the signs.
6. One sign said: "Please don't take the wood." The other said: "Many visitors take the wood."
7. With "please don't," only 2 % of the pieces vanished. With "many visitors"... almost 8 %.
8. You see, we don't copy rules. We copy each other. "Many people steal" sounds like what everyone does, like a teacher saying, "Half this class cheated."
9. The sign didn't stop thieves. It gave them permission.
10. And the bait wood? It came from the park's "conscience pile": pieces people stole, then mailed back with apology letters. So some of it got stolen twice.

## 4. Ricks Clips, "Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work" (30M, 65 s)

1. He sawed gaps into a railroad every 39 feet.
2. You see, two companies are racing to build a railroad along a canyon, and whoever finishes first gets the whole country's business.
3. He's the youngest engineer on the job, and when his boss sees a finger-wide gap between every single rail, his face goes purple: "You're wrecking my railroad!"
4. But he won't close a single one.
5. The other company lays their rails end to end, perfectly smooth, and finishes first; their trains glide across the canyon without a sound, while his go clickety-clack over every gap.
6. The newspapers start calling it the clickety-clack line, and everyone switches to the quiet trains.
7. Then summer comes, and something weird happens: his gaps start shrinking. In May, you can fit a coin in them; by July, barely a sheet of paper, and his boss is thrilled: "See? Even your gaps know you were wrong."
8. Meanwhile, on the perfect railroad, the rails have started groaning in the afternoon sun, pushing against each other like they want to get out.
9. On the hottest day anyone can remember, his gaps finally close. Rail touching rail.
10. And halfway across the canyon, the perfect railroad kicks sideways like a snake, right as a train rolls onto it.

## 5. Ricks Clips, "The Instinct That Makes You Crash" (10M, 59 s; the one our readers ranked first most often)

1. If you plant a tree beside an empty road, cars will steer right into it.
2. You see, the road runs dead straight for miles, and the only thing beside it is this one tree. But somehow, somebody hits it every week.
3. So the town nails a giant flashing arrow to it so nobody can miss it. Yet the crashes double.
4. The tow truck guy starts parking out there with a lawn chair, and he's never made more money in his life.
5. Eventually, the sheriff drives out to see what's going on; he goes 15 miles an hour with both hands on the wheel, and slams right into it.
6. He climbs out furious, grabs an ax off the tow truck, and chops the thing down, while the tow truck guy watches his whole business fall over.
7. The second his cruiser's fixed, the sheriff drives back out to enjoy his nice empty road, and plows straight into the stump.
8. So how does this keep happening? Well, it's called target fixation.
9. After miles of nothing, that tree sticks out like a lighthouse, especially with a giant flashing arrow pointing at it, so every driver's eyes lock onto it and won't let go.
10. And while they stare, their hands slowly turn the wheel toward it. Even when it's just a stump.

## Verdict (honest)

**What the two Ricks stories have that ours does not, read aloud:** each is ONE line of pressure. One person, one
place, one question, and every sentence makes it worse ("Yet the crashes double", "everyone switches", the gaps
shrink, the sheriff hits the stump). Someone is sure he is right (the boss, the sheriff), and the last sentence lands
on him. Ours is three true short stories in a row (the lab, the steam cab, New York), joined by "So". Each one has a
good laugh, but nothing grows across them. Sentence 7 of each Ricks story is the tightest; our sentence 7 starts a
new story. That is the material, not the wording, and it is why I rate both of ours below both references for grip.

**v5 against v4:** the same jokes and the same order, so it is as funny as v4 and no funnier. It is a little more
gripping in three places, all at the joins: sentence 4 now has a setback ("but five press anyway"); sentence 6 says
"So" instead of a lecture-like "which is why"; and sentence 7 turns the page ("But the shouting didn't start as
science.") at exactly the point where four of five readers said they would swipe. It is also about 4 s shorter on
the same voice, which on its own helps the 35-45 s dip. Read aloud, sentence 3 of v4 ("A hundred lights. Then,
red.") has more drama than v5's "A hundred lights, then red."; that is the one place where v4 reads better.

**Against anti-theft:** about level on laughs (both have about five or six; ours: "She did.", five pressed, not one,
the cereal, "He never knew about the eyes.", the sign, "had to point"). Anti-theft is tighter as a story: one woman,
one sign, one test and a twist on the same wood. Ours keeps changing subject. Ours has the stronger start (a game
that catches you) and the stronger end (it sends you back to replay).

**Is v5 as gripping and as funny as the references?** As funny: close (Instinct has six laughs in 59 s; v5 has six or
seven in 67 s). As gripping: no. The weakest stretch is still sentence 6-7, from the cereal (about 27 s) to the bad
eyes (about 42 s): about 14 s with no laugh, against 15 s in v4. No wording fix closes it fully, because the lab story
is over there; only a picture gag (the kid copying the driver's point with a big grin, already in the shot) or a new
true laugh can.

**Would I keep v4 anywhere?** Yes, in three places, and none costs a picture:
- Line 4: if the readers miss the drama, keep v4's two beats "A hundred lights. Then, red." (+0.8 s; v5 has 0.6 s
  spare under 68 s, so it would then need reserve 1, dropping "You see,").
- Lines 5, 7, 8: if the present tense reads oddly to fresh readers, v4's past forms ("watched", "pressed") are
  already recorded and fit the same time.
- Line 22 is v4's, unchanged.
Everything else in v5 I would keep over v4.
