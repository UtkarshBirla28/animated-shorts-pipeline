# Read 6: Shorts strategist review (blind)

Counting at about 3 words a second. Single shouted words ("Go.") at about 2 a second.

## P (65 s): railroad gaps
1. "He sawed gaps into a railroad every 39 feet." By 3 s the whole line is spoken. By 5 s: "...You see, two companies are racing to build". It promises a strange act and a reason, so most people won't swipe. One weakness: "He" is nobody yet, so the first picture has to carry it.
2. Loop: why the gaps, and will he be proven right? Shrinking gaps plus groaning rails build it well.
3. Comments: "thermal expansion, obviously". The know-it-alls comment to prove they knew it, and others ask "did the train crash?"
4. It ends on a cliffhanger, not a loop line. You get comments, but few rewatches.
5. Stay about 70%, average viewed about 80%. **7.5**

## Q (68 s): pointing, opens on the fact
1. "Japan's railway lab flashed a hundred lights at people, just to catch them on one red." By 3 s: "Japan's railway lab flashed a hundred lights at". The hook word "red" only lands at about 5.5 s. Then "Try it." switches to a game. So it pays the homework cost anyway, and its hook is slower too.
2. Loop: "Did you tap?", then the origin story, then the New York sign.
3. Comments: "I tapped", the cereal in the fridge, "dead sexy".
4. "Now try again" asks for a rewatch, but the replay restarts on the fact sentence, not on the game, so the loop has a 5 s lag.
5. Stay about 60%, average viewed about 70%. **6**

## R (74 s): count the phones / clown
1. "Quick test. Count the people on a phone." That is 8 words, all spoken by 3 s. By 5 s it is silence over a busy square. The task is easy and visual, but 7 s with no voice is a gamble in the feed. It survives only if the square is busy and readable at once.
2. Loop: "And the clown?" at about 12 s is a strong early payoff. Hyman's section in the middle is talky and sags.
3. Comments: "I counted six", "what clown??", "I saw him".
4. "Did you see the money?" is the best rewatch trigger in the pack. It sends people back to the opening scene. That only works if the money really is visible there.
5. Stay about 68%. Average viewed about 90%, because rewatches push it up. **7.5**

## S (71 s): pointing, opens on the task, with "1994"
1. "Tap your foot on every green light. Only green." That is 9 words by 3 s. By 5 s you are into "Go. Go. Go.", and the red trap comes at about 6 to 7 s. The task costs the viewer almost nothing, and it pays off in seconds.
2. Loop: the same as Q.
3. Comments: the same as Q.
4. The loop is seamless: "point, and say it" leads straight back into the green lights.
5. Losses: "In 1994", "A hundred lights. Then, red.", and the clipped past tense all slow the middle. "And where did it come from?" is a flat turn. It runs 4 s longer than V.
6. Stay about 68%, average viewed about 72%. **6.5**

## T (59 s): tree on an empty road
1. "If you plant a tree beside an empty road, cars will steer right into it." By 3 s: "If you plant a tree beside an empty road". The whole absurd promise is in by 5 s. Strong.
2. Loop: "how?" The escalation keeps it going: the flashing arrow, then the tow-truck guy in his lawn chair, then the sheriff, then the stump. It's funny, and every beat raises the stakes.
3. Comments: bikers saying "target fixation is real", plus "is this a true story?"
4. "Even when it's just a stump." is a clean button, but it isn't a loop. The short length does the work instead.
5. Stay about 72%, average viewed about 85%. **8**

## U (83 s): petrified forest sign
1. "She'd never stolen anything." By 3 s, "She'd never stolen anything. Not even a", which is generic. By 5 s, "...paperclip. Then she read one sign". The real hook ("wanted to steal") arrives at about 6 to 7 s, after pauses. Swipe risk is high.
2. Loop: why a warning makes people steal. It's solid, but it takes a long time to get there.
3. Comments: the "half this class cheated" line, and confessions about taking rocks.
4. "So some of it got stolen twice." is a great kicker that people will repeat, but it isn't a loop.
5. At 83 s it is too long, and average viewed suffers. Stay about 55%, average viewed about 62%. **5.5**

## V (67 s): pointing, opens on the task, tight
1. Same opening as S: the trap pays off at about 6 s.
2. Loop: "But the shouting didn't start as science." is the best turn of the three versions.
3. Comments: "I tapped", "dead sexy". The prank is the share bait, and it comes late, which helps people stay.
4. It has the best loop of the three. The last line hands the viewer straight back to the green lights.
5. The back half is still three loosely linked anecdotes (the lab, the half-blind driver, New York).
6. Stay about 70%, average viewed about 80%. **7**

## The three versions of one film (Q, S, V)
I would post V, with the task opening. Here the "homework" fear is overstated. Tapping a foot is a reflex, not a chore. The trap springs at about 6 s, which is an earlier reward than any fact sentence can give. And a play-along opening makes the "Now try again" ending a true loop. Q's fact line takes about 5.5 s to land and then asks for the task anyway. It carries the homework risk without the fast payoff, and it breaks the loop.

The risk I accept: anyone who reads "Tap your foot" as a task and swipes in the first 2 s is lost for good. That makes the first frame critical. It must show a big green light and the girl as the line starts, not a title card or an empty scene.

## Wording changes to V (no new facts, no extra length)
1. "Did you tap? / She did." becomes **"You tapped. / So did she."** It's an accusation, not a question. That provokes "no I didn't" comments, and it is no longer.
2. "Now try again. / This time, point, and say it." becomes **"Now again. / Point, and say it."** It cuts dead air before the restart, so the loop snaps back into the green lights.
3. "Twelve people just watch, but five press anyway." becomes **"Twelve people just watch. Five still press."** It's tighter, and the number lands as its own beat.
