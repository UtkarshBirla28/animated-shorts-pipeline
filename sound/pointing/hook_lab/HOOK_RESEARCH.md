# What stops a thumb: the first 0-5 seconds of a Short (hook research)

Research note for the studio, 2026-11-19. Written for the `pointing` Short (script: `../SCRIPT_v2.md`) and every story
Short after it. Every claim carries a source (list at the end). Where I go beyond the sources, I write **(guess)**.

**Evidence grades used below**

| grade | meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Many lab experiments agree and the mechanism is well understood. The step from the lab to a phone feed is still my inference. |
| **B** | Large real-world data: field experiments or platform data with big samples. Usually ads or headlines, not Shorts themselves. |
| **C** | Small studies, vendor or creator data, methods not published, or results that disagree with each other. |
| **D** | My own reasoning or common craft practice. A guess to test, not a finding. |

---

## 0. The answer in ten lines

1. **No hook stops all viewers.** In Paddy Galloway's analysis of 5,400 Shorts, the best Shorts kept **70-90 %** of
   viewers ("viewed vs swiped away"), and Shorts under 60 % rarely did well. Our clown Short kept 68 %. A realistic
   goal for `pointing` is **75-80 %**, not 100 %.
2. The swipe decision is fast. On Facebook's mobile feed, people spent **1.7 s** on an average item, and **0.25 s** was
   enough for them to remember it later (Facebook IQ, 2016). The brain gets the gist of a picture in **13-150 ms** and
   moves the eyes to a face in about **100-140 ms**.
3. So **frame 0 is the thumbnail.** In the Shorts feed, YouTube plays the video, not your custom cover. The cover
   only shows in search, on the channel page and on the home page.
4. **Seeing comes first (0-300 ms).** What grabs the eye before you think: something starting to move, something
   rushing toward you, a face, a pointing finger, a sudden sound that lands with a visual hit, and a human voice.
5. **The decision comes next (300 ms-2 s).** People stay if they can name what they are looking at and something in
   it breaks their expectations. That means a question they want answered, a person who stands to lose something, or
   someone who is about to be proven wrong.
6. **Surprise predicts where people look.** In video, 72 % of eye movements went to spots more surprising than
   average (Itti & Baldi). The brain's reward system fires for novelty, and curiosity (a gap in what you know)
   improves memory and makes people pay to find the answer.
7. **People swipe** when the start is slow, opens on a logo or brand, has no clear subject, shows a text wall, or is
   something they have already seen. Brand-first starts measurably raise zapping in ad studies. "Seen it" kills
   curiosity: you are not curious when you are sure you know the answer.
8. **State the result, don't ask a quiz.** Results told in advance ("spoilers") did not spoil twist or mystery stories;
   people enjoyed them more (Leavitt & Christenfeld, n = 819). Our own two data points agree: the "it made people
   steal MORE" result-first hook did best, and the quiz hook lost 32 % of viewers. Headline data on questions is mixed.
9. **Plan for sound-off and for loops.** A large share of feed viewing is muted, and captions raised Facebook video
   view time by 12 %. Since 31 March 2025, every replay of a Short counts as a view.
10. The 20 rules are in section 9, ranked. **The five with the strongest evidence:** motion in frame 0, one clear focal
    point, a face or pointing gaze aimed at the hook, no brand or intro first, and an open question by about 2 s.

## 1. How the decision unfolds, millisecond by millisecond

This timeline combines lab timing data with platform data. The stages and their order are well supported. The exact
edges between them are my estimate **(guess)**.

| time | what the brain is doing | what wins here | key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| during the swipe | The next Short slides in. Frame 0 is seen while it is still moving. | A bright, simple, high-contrast frame 0 | Observation of the app, not documented **(guess)** |
| 0-150 ms | Automatic capture, before any thinking | Something starting to move, onsets, something rushing toward you, faces, sudden sounds | Abrams & Christ 2003; Franconeri & Simons 2003; Crouzet et al. 2010 (saccades to faces from 100 ms); Thorpe et al. 1996 |
| 100-300 ms | Gist: "what kind of scene is this?" | One subject that reads at a glance | Potter et al. 2014 (meaning from 13 ms pictures) |
| 300 ms-1.7 s | Is this worth my next few seconds? | Novelty, surprise, a person with stakes, an open question | Facebook IQ 1.7 s dwell; Itti & Baldi 2005; Bunzeck & Duzel 2006 |
| 1.5-5 s | Re-checking: is this as good as what I'd get by swiping? | A second beat that raises the question or the stakes | Amplified Intelligence: attention drops off fast after 1.5-2 s on fast feeds; most skips come in the first 5 s (Lee et al. 2025) |
| 5 s and on | Story mode | Characters, escalation, jokes, a promised payoff | Teixeira et al. 2012 (joy sustains viewing); Green & Brock 2000 (being absorbed in a story) |

**A useful model: the feed is a foraging ground.** Animals leave a feeding patch when it yields less than the average
patch nearby (Charnov's marginal value theorem; Pirolli & Card applied it to people looking for information). A
viewer leaves a Short when the reward they expect from staying drops below what they expect from the next swipe.
The next swipe costs one flick, so the bar is high. This explains why "seen it" and "slow" kill a Short: both lower the
reward the viewer expects from staying. No one has tested this model on Short swipes directly. The theory is grade
A in ecology, but applying it to Shorts is a **(guess)**.

**"Stop all viewers at once" has a scientific name: attentional synchrony.** When a film is tightly directed, all
viewers look at the same place and their brains respond alike. In fMRI, a Hitchcock episode made about **65 %** of the
cortex respond in sync across viewers. A Sergio Leone film reached about 45 %, *Curb Your Enthusiasm* 18 %, and
unedited footage of a park **under 5 %** (Hasson et al. 2008). In eye-tracking of video, motion predicted the moments
when all viewers' eyes bunched in one place (Mital et al. 2011). Moments when brains respond alike across viewers also
predicted how large audiences rated TV scenes and Super Bowl ads (Dmochowski et al. 2014: r = 0.4 with tweet rate). So
the honest version of the owner's goal is this: **make every viewer look at the same thing at the same moment, and make
that thing surprising.**

## 2. Before thinking (0-300 ms): what grabs the eye and ear

| factor | finding | size / detail | grade | what it means for us |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Motion onset** (something starting to move) | Something that starts to move grabs attention. Motion that is already going on does not grab it as strongly (Abrams & Christ 2003). Sudden appearances grab attention too (Yantis & Jonides 1984). | People react faster to targets that just started moving, and finding them does not slow down when the screen is cluttered | A | Frame 0 should be the *start* of a movement (the glove snapping up), not the middle of a long, steady pan. |
| **Looming** (something rushing toward you) | Objects moving and growing toward you grab attention. Objects moving away do not (Franconeri & Simons 2003). This fits a "behavioural urgency" idea: what might hit you gets priority. | Shown in lab search tasks. Later work argues about whether objects moving away also count. | A | A push-in, or something moving toward the lens, in the first second. |
| **Animate motion** | Objects that move by themselves, as animals do, grab attention more than objects pushed by outside forces (Pratt et al. 2010, six experiments). | Faster responses to the targets that moved by themselves | A | Characters should act (snap, laugh, point), not just be moved around by the camera. |
| **Faces** | People's eyes jump to faces from **100-110 ms**, about 140 ms on average, even when told to look for vehicles instead (Crouzet et al. 2010). | Fast enough to be partly involuntary | A | A readable face with a clear expression in frame 0 or by 0.5 s. |
| **Eye contact** | A face looking straight at you changes how your brain processes everything that follows (the "eye contact effect"; Senju & Johnson 2009). | Review paper; strong infant and adult evidence | A (lab) / D (for a cartoon looking at the lens) | Use eye contact sparingly. A character glancing at the lens at the hook line is a test candidate **(guess)**. |
| **A pointing finger** | A picture of a pointing index finger moves viewers' attention to where it points, reflexively, and more strongly than other hand shapes (Ariga & Watanabe 2009). Where others point and look also shifts attention automatically (Langton & Bruce 2000). | The effect shows at short delays (it is reflexive). It shrinks if the finger's shape is changed. | A | **Our topic is a pointing finger.** The glove in frame 0 is a built-in attention arrow. Make sure it points at something the viewer can find. |
| **High contrast / colour singleton** (the only item of its colour) | An item with a unique colour can grab attention (Theeuwes 1992). This depends on what the viewer is looking for (Folk et al. 1992). A sudden onset is more reliable than colour alone. | Lab results are mixed and depend on the viewer's goal | A (lab), C (feed) | The yellow suitcase as the only saturated yellow in the film tags the payoff object. It helps but will not carry the hook alone. |
| **Threat** | Angry or threatening faces stand out in a crowd faster than friendly ones (Ohman et al. 2001). | Small, reliable speed advantages | A | Danger or loss works, but our tone is comic. Use "about to lose something", not real danger. |
| **Sudden sound onset** | New sounds pull attention involuntarily (Escera et al. 1998). A sound that gets louder, like something approaching, acts as a warning and holds attention longer than one that fades (Neuhoff; Ghazanfar et al. 2002). | Measured with brain signals and reaction times | A | A real sound event at 0-200 ms, not a silent start or a slow fade-in of music. |
| **Sound synced to a visual change** ("pip and pop") | A brief tone timed exactly with a visual change makes that change pop out. Searching for it became far faster, even though the tone gave no location information (Van der Burg et al. 2008). | Very large drops in search time in the lab | A | Sync the first sound hit to the first visual hit, within one frame. The snap SFX lands on the glove. |
| **A human voice** | Listeners recognised voices faster than instrument sounds (Agus et al. 2012). The brain has regions that respond specifically to voices. | Faster reaction times for voices | A (lab), C (feed) | Lead with a voice line (the shout), not a music sting. Use music to set the mood under the voice. |
| **Sound on vs off, overall** | Ads seen *and* heard got **2.4x** more attention than ads shown with the sound off and subtitles (Ipsos/Google eye-tracking, 2017). **88 %** of TikTok users said sound is essential (Kantar for TikTok, 2021). | Platform-funded studies | B/C | On Shorts, sound is a big lever, but frame 0 must still work silent (section 5). |

## 3. The decision (300 ms-2 s): what makes people *choose* to stay

| factor | finding | size / detail | grade | what it means for us |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Surprise / prediction error** (when what happens differs from what the brain expected) | "Bayesian surprise" (how much a moment changes what you expect) predicted where people looked in TV and game footage better than any other measure: **72 %** of eye movements went to more-surprising-than-average spots, and **84 %** where four viewers agreed (Itti & Baldi 2005). Dopamine neurons signal "better or worse than expected" (Schultz et al. 1997). | Strong | A | The first line or image should break an expectation, not confirm one. |
| **Novelty** | The brain's midbrain reward area (SN/VTA) responded to stimulus novelty itself, not to rarity, negative emotion or task importance (Bunzeck & Duzel 2006). | fMRI, two studies | A | A *new* thing beats a *loud* thing. |
| **Curiosity gap** (Loewenstein's information-gap theory) | Curiosity is a felt gap between what you know and what you want to know (Loewenstein 1994). In fMRI, curiosity lit up the brain's reward areas (caudate). People spent scarce tokens or waiting time to get answers, and remembered surprising answers better 1-2 weeks later (Kang et al. 2009). In a curious state, people even remembered unrelated faces better a day later (Gruber et al. 2014). | Curiosity peaks when you **half know**: it is an inverted U against how sure you are of the answer (Kang et al. 2009) | A | The hook must raise a question the viewer *can* guess at but cannot be sure of. |
| **"Forward reference"** (headline language that points to something not yet shown) | In thousands of Upworthy headline A/B tests, language that pointed forward to unseen content raised clicks (Banerjee & Urminsky, 2021 working paper). | Field experiments; exact sizes are in the paper | B | Words like "Give him a minute..." point forward. Good. |
| **Zeigarnik / open loop** | The famous claim, that unfinished tasks are remembered better, **failed** a 2025 meta-analysis. What held up is the urge to *resume* an interrupted task (the Ovsiankina effect; Ghibellini & Meier 2025). | No memory advantage found | C (as usually told) | Do not rely on "open loops are remembered". Rely on "people want to finish what they started", which is weaker but real. |
| **Outcome first** ("spoilers") | Readers enjoyed ironic-twist and mystery stories **more** when told the ending first. Literary stories trended the same way (Leavitt & Christenfeld 2011, n = 819). | Significant for twist and mystery stories | B (enjoyment, not retention) | Stating the surprising outcome first is supported. The curiosity moves from "what happens?" to "how?". |
| **Stakes / negativity** | In 22,743 Upworthy headline experiments, each extra negative word raised clicks by **2.3 %**, and each positive word lowered them by **1.0 %** (Robertson et al. 2023). | Large field data (370M impressions) | B | Give a named person something to lose, early. |
| **Social conflict / someone wronged or mocked** | Each word about the out-group (the "other side") raised the odds of a share by **67 %** (Rathje et al. 2021, 2.7M posts). Each moral-emotional word raised retweets by about **20 %** (Brady et al. 2017). Headlines implying social stories about individuals did better on Upworthy (Banerjee & Urminsky). | Sharing data, not watch time | B (sharing), C (staying) | A mocker who will be proven wrong is a strong engine, as long as the joke is on him and not on Japan. |
| **In medias res** (starting in the middle of the action) | No direct test of "start mid-action" on swipe rates that I could find. The support is indirect: motion onset, surprise, and getting absorbed in a story (Green & Brock 2000). | n/a | D (direct), A (parts) | Start mid-action anyway. Every part of the mechanism is supported. |
| **Specific numbers and odd details** | Headlines with numbers raised clicks on Upworthy (Banerjee & Urminsky, working paper). Precise numbers make the speaker seem more confident (Jerez-Fernandez et al. 2014). Distinctive, odd details are remembered better (the von Restorff effect). | Small to moderate | C | "Over a thousand people", "six to one", a *yellow* suitcase. Use concrete details over general ones. |
| **Questions vs statements** | Mixed. In two small field experiments, question headlines got far more clicks (+150 % on Twitter; +137 % to +257 % on a shop site; Lai & Farbrot 2014). In thousands of Upworthy tests, headlines speaking straight to the reader ("you") **backfired** (Banerjee & Urminsky). | Results disagree | C | A quiz asks the viewer to work before any reward. Our quiz hook lost 32 %. If we use a question, it should come *after* a visual surprise, not as the first beat. |
| **Early surprise, then steady joy** | In ads, a quick surprise at the start, then a long stretch of joy, kept the most attention. Rising joy reduced zapping (switching away) more than falling joy increased it (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012; eye tracking plus face reading at 250 ms steps). | Lab-field ad data | B | Do not save the only surprise for the end. Surprise at 0-3 s, then a laugh every few seconds. |
| **Laughter on screen** | Adding real laughter after a joke made people rate it funnier. Real laughter worked better than canned laughter (Cai et al. 2019). | Moderate | B (lab) | The tourist's *real* laugh at 1 s helps the gag land. Record a genuine laugh, not a "ha-ha" read. |

## 4. What makes people swipe

| cause | evidence | grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand, logo or intro first** | In zapping data from nearly 2,000 viewers, a brand shown in the centre of the screen *raised* switching away. Short repeated brand flashes cut zapping by up to **25 %** compared with one long brand shot (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2010). At Google, ads with the brand in the first 5 s got better recall but **more skips**, and a logo that just "pops up" lowered view-through (Unskippable Labs). A mobile cut with no brand-led structure was viewed **26 %** more but recalled 54 % less. | B |
| **Scattered attention / no clear subject** | In the same 2010 study, scattered viewer attention (eyes all over the frame) "strongly" predicted switching away. Hasson's unedited park footage synced brains the least (under 5 %). | B (A for the mechanism) |
| **Slow start / slow reveal** | Google's YouTube playbook warns that the slow reveals of TV ads lead to an "immediate skip". TikTok found that over **63 %** of its highest click-rate videos showed the key message in the first 3 s (correlational; method not published). | B/C |
| **"Seen it" topic** | You are least curious when you are sure you know the answer (Kang et al. 2009, inverted U). Novelty, not loudness, drives the brain's reward response (Bunzeck & Duzel 2006). No platform data on repeated topics exists publicly. | A (mechanism) / D (size on Shorts) |
| **Text walls** | People spend about 1.7 s per feed item. Typical silent reading runs about 4 words per second (Rayner et al. 2016). Text in print ads drew attention only in proportion to the space it took up, while pictures captured attention whatever their size (Pieters & Wedel 2004, 1,363 ads). English subtitle standards cap reading speed at about 20 characters per second for adults and 17 for children (Netflix, as widely reported). | B |
| **Talking before anything happens** | No direct study. It follows from the motion-onset and synchrony findings, and from our own clown cut 1 being rejected for a static camera. | D |
| **Low contrast or dark first frame** | Brightness contrast predicts where people look only weakly. Motion and flicker predict it far better (Mital et al. 2011). Some creator guides say the feed may pick another frame if the first one is dark or blank (not confirmed by YouTube). | C |
| **Recognition as an ad or "content"** | Not tested for Shorts. People skip what looks like advertising (Google's ad work above), and our owner's notes reject anything that looks "prototype-grade". | D |

## 5. Sound-off viewers

- **How many?** No current YouTube Shorts figure is published. In a 2019 Verizon Media/Publicis survey of 5,616 US
  adults, 69 % said they watch video with the sound off in public. 80 % said captions make them more likely to finish
  a video (both answers are what people *said*, not measured behaviour). TikTok's culture is sound-on (88 % say sound
  is essential). My estimate for Shorts: mostly sound-on, with a meaningful muted minority (commuters, offices,
  bedtime) **(guess)**.
- **What helps:** Facebook's internal tests found captions raised average video view time by **12 %** (2016). Facebook
  had its raters judge the first 10 s of ads **muted** to mimic the feed. Google's playbook asks for title cards that
  "make sense with the sound off".
- **For us:** Frame 0 to 3 s must tell the story to a muted viewer through picture plus captions. Big caption chunks
  of 3 words or fewer, call-outs in CAPS (already in v2), digits on screen. A cut must never depend on a sound joke the
  muted viewer cannot see. Grade **B** for captions, **D** for the exact share.

## 6. The loop and the rewatch

- **What YouTube counts:** Since 31 March 2025, a Shorts *view* is counted every time a Short starts or replays.
  *Engaged views* (the old metric, used for the Partner Program) count only viewers who "stayed to watch past the
  initial seconds, not including any loops" (YouTube Help). Average percentage viewed can pass 100 % when people
  rewatch.
- **Evidence that loops help ranking:** None published by YouTube. Creator reports: Jenny Hoyos says that on her
  channel, Shorts under 30 s need **over 100 %** average viewed to take off, and that cutting one second once raised her
  retention from 83 % to 88 % (interview summaries, an anecdote).
- **Why it works (mechanism):** If the last frame flows into frame 0, a viewer who does not notice the cut re-watches
  the hook for free. A hook that rewards a second look ("wait, the bag was there at the start") turns that free second
  view into real watch time. Grade **B** for the counting rules, **C** for the effect on ranking.
- **For us:** v2 already ends on the phone screen cutting into frame 0. Keep it.

## 7. What YouTube says counts

| metric | YouTube's definition (Help Center) | what it measures for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Stayed to watch** (the old label "viewed vs swiped away" is the same idea) | "The percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short." The cut-off in seconds is **not published**. | **The hook.** This is the number the first 3-5 s control. |
| **Engaged views** | "How many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops." | Hook times reach. Used for the Partner Program. |
| **Average view duration / average percentage viewed** | Calculated "among those who stayed to watch", that is, from engaged views. | **The body and the payoff**, *not* the hook. A great hook can sit beside a weak average viewed, and the reverse. |
| **Views** | Every start or replay, since 31 March 2025. | Inflated by loops. Do not compare with views from before April 2025. |

- **How reach is decided:** YouTube's Shorts product lead Todd Sherman (2023): "the audience is the algorithm". A Short
  is shown to some viewers first. If they watch, it gets more traffic; if they swipe, traffic tapers. A view should
  "encode for your intent of watching". Thresholds are kept private to prevent gaming. The stated goal is viewer
  satisfaction.
- **First frame vs cover:** YouTube has said thumbnails matter less for Shorts because most viewers find Shorts in the
  feed, where the video plays itself (Social Media Today, 2023). Custom covers appear in search, on the channel page,
  in subscriptions and on the home page. **Practical rule: design frame 0 as the real thumbnail. Design the cover for
  people browsing the channel.**

## 9. The 20 hook rules, ranked

**How they are ranked:** expected effect on *stayed to watch* for an animated story Short, weighted by evidence
strength. Rules 1-10 are the core. Rules 11-20 refine them. Each rule comes with a test you can run on a draft
*before* publishing. The measurement commands are in section 11.

| # | rule (short) | grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Frame 0 is already mid-action: motion starts in the first 200 ms | A |
| 2 | One subject, one place to look, readable at a glance | A/B |
| 3 | Result first: say or show the surprising outcome by about 3 s | B |
| 4 | Open one nameable question by about 2 s; answer it late | A/B |
| 5 | Lead with the new angle, not the famous fact | A mechanism / D size |
| 6 | A face with a clear emotion, or a pointing hand, aimed at the hook object | A |
| 7 | One person with something concrete to lose by 3 s | B |
| 8 | No logo, title card, channel intro, greeting or fade | B |
| 9 | Sound in the first 200 ms, synced to the first visual hit; a voice by 0.5 s | A (lab) |
| 10 | The hook works on mute: picture plus big captions | B |
| 11 | 5 words of text or fewer on screen in 0-1.5 s | B |
| 12 | A bright, high-contrast frame 0, with one colour singleton on the key object | A/C |
| 13 | Never open with a quiz, a task or a "you" question | C |
| 14 | A doubter who is about to be proven wrong (punch up at him) | B/C |
| 15 | A surprise in the first 3 s, then a laugh every few seconds | B |
| 16 | A second hook between 3 and 5 s | C |
| 17 | Push in, or have something move toward the lens, in the first second | A |
| 18 | One concrete number or odd detail in the first 5 s | C |
| 19 | No narration over a static picture in 0-5 s | D |
| 20 | The ending flows back into frame 0 | B/C |

### Rule 1. Frame 0 is already mid-action
- **Rule:** The first frame is the *start* of a movement that completes in 0.2-0.5 s: a snap, a fall, a door, a laugh.
  No fade from black, no still establishing shot, no slow camera drift as the only motion.
- **Mechanism:** Motion onset and sudden appearances grab attention automatically. Motion is the best predictor of
  all viewers looking at the same spot.
- **Evidence:** A (Abrams & Christ 2003; Yantis & Jonides 1984; Pratt et al. 2010; Mital et al. 2011).
- **Measure:** Frame-to-frame change (ffmpeg `YDIF`) in frames 1-12 is at least the clip's median, and frame 0 is not
  black (section 11). By eye: pause on frame 0 and check that something is visibly about to move.

### Rule 2. One subject, one place to look
- **Rule:** In 0-3 s every shot has a single focal object, large (at least about a fifth of the frame height), with a
  clean background. No busy wide shots in the hook.
- **Mechanism:** The brain gets the gist in 13-150 ms. Scattered attention predicts switching away. Tight direction
  syncs viewers' brains.
- **Evidence:** A/B (Potter et al. 2014; Thorpe et al. 1996; Teixeira et al. 2010; Hasson et al. 2008).
- **Measure:** **Flash test:** show frame 0 (and the frame at 1.5 s) for 0.25 s to 5 people and ask "what did you
  see?". Pass: at least 4 of 5 name the same subject. **Tap test:** "tap where you looked": at least 4 of 5 tap within
  the same tenth of the screen.

### Rule 3. Result first
- **Rule:** By about 3 s, say or show an outcome that contradicts what the viewer expects ("...and it made people
  steal MORE"). The question then becomes *how*, not *what*.
- **Mechanism:** Prediction error grabs attention. Knowing the outcome adds a frame for following the story and does
  not cost enjoyment. A "how" gap stays open until the explanation.
- **Evidence:** B (Leavitt & Christenfeld 2011; Itti & Baldi 2005; our anti-theft vs clown, n = 2).
- **Measure:** The first or second spoken sentence holds a concrete outcome. **5-person test after 3 s:** "Do you know
  how that happened?" (want: no) and "Do you want to?" (want: at least 4 of 5 yes).

### Rule 4. Open one nameable question by about 2 s, and answer it late
- **Rule:** By 2 s a viewer can say, in one sentence, what they are waiting to find out. The full answer lands after
  about 60 % of the runtime, with smaller answers along the way.
- **Mechanism:** An information gap drives the brain's reward-seeking. Curiosity peaks when the viewer half-knows
  the answer.
- **Evidence:** A (Loewenstein 1994; Kang et al. 2009; Gruber et al. 2014), plus B (forward-reference headlines).
- **Measure:** Stop the draft at 2.5 s and ask 5 people "what do you want to find out?". Pass: at least 4 of 5 give
  the same question. Note the timestamp of the answer in the script table.

### Rule 5. Lead with the new angle, not the famous fact
- **Rule:** If the topic has been viral before (this one has: Atlas Obscura, 99% Invisible, Nippon.com, many
  explainers), the first 3 s must show the part viewers *haven't* seen. Here that is the tourist's suitcase, the New
  York twist, or the boy, not "Japanese drivers point".
- **Mechanism:** When people are sure they know the answer, curiosity drops. Novelty, not loudness, drives the
  reward response. The foraging model says "seen it" means a low expected payoff, so the viewer swipes.
- **Evidence:** A for the mechanism (Kang et al. 2009; Bunzeck & Duzel 2006). How big this is on Shorts: D.
- **Measure:** **Seen-it test:** after 3 s, ask "have you seen a video about this before?". If at least 2 of 5 say yes
  *and* say they know where it is going, rewrite the hook to lead with the unfamiliar element.

### Rule 6. A face with a clear emotion, or a pointing hand, aimed at the hook object
- **Rule:** Frame 0 has a readable face (a big, clear expression) or a hand doing something, and the gaze or the
  pointing finger leads the eye to the key object.
- **Mechanism:** People's eyes jump to faces in about 100 ms. A pointing index finger shifts attention reflexively.
  Photos with faces drew more likes.
- **Evidence:** A (Crouzet et al. 2010; Ariga & Watanabe 2009; Langton & Bruce 2000), B (Bakhshi et al. 2014: +38 %
  likes, n = 1.1M; Vidmob/TikTok: someone handling the product raised 6-second view-through by 17 %).
- **Measure:** Frame-0 checklist: face height at least about 10 % of the frame, or the hand is in clear silhouette.
  Draw a line along the finger or the gaze: does it hit the hook object? Tap test as in rule 2.

### Rule 7. One person with something concrete to lose by 3 s
- **Rule:** By 3 s the viewer knows *who* could lose *what* (a suitcase, a bet, their pride), told about one person,
  not people in general.
- **Mechanism:** Negativity and loss raise engagement. Stories about one named individual beat abstract claims.
- **Evidence:** B (Robertson et al. 2023: +2.3 % clicks per negative word, 22,743 tests; Banerjee & Urminsky: social
  stories about individuals did better).
- **Measure:** Stop at 3 s and ask "who might lose something here, and what?". Pass: at least 4 of 5 answer
  correctly.

### Rule 8. No logo, title card, channel intro, greeting or fade
- **Rule:** The first story event is in frame 0. Branding, if any, comes later and briefly, woven into the scene.
- **Mechanism:** Brand-first starts raise switching away. Logos that pop up lower view-through.
- **Evidence:** B (Teixeira et al. 2010; Google Unskippable Labs; Google playbook).
- **Measure:** Count the frames before the first story event. Pass: 0.

### Rule 9. Sound in the first 200 ms, synced to the first visual hit, and a voice by 0.5 s
- **Rule:** A crisp sound event (snap, chime, shout) starts by 0.2 s and lands on the first visual change, within 1
  frame. A human voice starts by about 0.5 s. Music sits under the voice; it does not open alone.
- **Mechanism:** Sudden sounds pull attention. A sound synced with a visual change makes the change pop out. Voices
  are recognised faster than instruments.
- **Evidence:** A in the lab (Escera et al. 1998; Van der Burg et al. 2008; Agus et al. 2012). C for the feed (Ipsos
  2.4x attention with sound).
- **Measure:** `silencedetect` gives the first sound at 0.2 s or earlier (section 11). Check that the snap sound sits
  on the glove's snap frame in the editor. Voice start comes from the script timing.

### Rule 10. The hook works on mute
- **Rule:** Picture plus captions alone tell a muted viewer who, what, and why it is odd.
- **Mechanism:** Many people watch muted. Captions keep people watching longer.
- **Evidence:** B (Facebook captions +12 % view time; Facebook IQ's muted 10-second test design; Verizon/Publicis
  survey; Google playbook).
- **Measure:** **Mute test:** 5 people watch 0-3 s muted, then explain what is going on. Pass: at least 4 of 5 get it
  right.

### Rule 11. 5 words of text or fewer on screen in 0-1.5 s
- **Rule:** Caption chunks of 3 words or fewer. At most one extra label. Never a sentence-long title on frame 0.
- **Mechanism:** A viewer has about 1.7 s and reads about 4 words per second, and text draws attention only in
  proportion to its size.
- **Evidence:** B (Facebook IQ 2016; Rayner et al. 2016; Pieters & Wedel 2004).
- **Measure:** Count the words on screen in 0-1.5 s (5 or fewer). Caption speed at most about 15 characters per
  second **(guess, kept stricter than Netflix's limit because viewers are also watching motion)**.

### Rule 12. A bright, high-contrast frame 0, with one colour singleton on the key object
- **Rule:** Frame 0 is mid-bright (no night or dim interior to open), the subject stands out from its background, and
  the payoff object is the only item of its colour (our yellow suitcase).
- **Mechanism:** Visual salience. A unique colour can grab attention, though less reliably than an onset. A dark
  frame 0 is low contrast against the dark feed interface.
- **Evidence:** A in the lab with caveats (Theeuwes 1992; Folk et al. 1992). C for the feed.
- **Measure:** Frame-0 mean brightness (ffmpeg `YAVG`) of at least about 90 on the 0-255 scale **(guess
  threshold)**. Check that there is no other saturated yellow in 0-5 s.

### Rule 13. Never open with a quiz, a task or a "you" question
- **Rule:** The first beat gives before it asks. A question, if any, comes *after* a visual surprise, around 2-4 s.
- **Mechanism:** A quiz asks for effort before any reward, and on a feed the next swipe is free. Speaking straight to
  the reader backfired in Upworthy tests.
- **Evidence:** C (mixed: Lai & Farbrot found question headlines got more clicks; Banerjee & Urminsky found
  direct address backfired; our quiz hook lost 32 %).
- **Measure:** Check that the script has no instruction or question to the viewer before about 2 s. If we ever test a
  quiz, run it against a result-first version with the feed-sandwich test (section 11).

### Rule 14. A doubter who is about to be proven wrong
- **Rule:** Early on, a likeable character mocks the thing the video will prove right. Their comeuppance is the
  payoff. Punch up at the doubter, never down at a culture.
- **Mechanism:** Social and moral emotions (someone wrong, someone smug) drive engagement and sharing. Schadenfreude
  creates a prediction the viewer wants to see come true.
- **Evidence:** B for sharing (Rathje et al. 2021: +67 % share odds per out-group word; Brady et al. 2017: +20 % per
  moral-emotional word). C for staying.
- **Measure:** By 3 s, is there a person who is clearly wrong and clearly going to pay? At the end, ask 5 people
  "who was the joke on?" (want: the tourist, not Japan).

### Rule 15. A surprise in the first 3 s, then a laugh every few seconds
- **Rule:** The first surprise lands by 3 s. After that, a small reward (a laugh, a reveal, a gag) at least every 5-8
  s **(guess interval)**. Never save the only surprise for the end.
- **Mechanism:** An early surprise plus sustained joy kept viewers watching. Real laughter on screen makes jokes land
  better.
- **Evidence:** B (Teixeira et al. 2012; Cai et al. 2019).
- **Measure:** Mark every reward beat on the script timeline. The longest gap between beats in 0-30 s should be 8 s or
  less. Panel: count laughs or smiles per 10 s.

### Rule 16. A second hook between 3 and 5 s
- **Rule:** Between 3 and 5 s something new happens that raises the question or the stakes (a new gag, a cut, a
  reveal). Don't coast on the first hook.
- **Mechanism:** On fast feeds attention drops off after 1.5-2 s, most skips come within 5 s, and the foraging
  re-check happens here.
- **Evidence:** C (Nelson-Field / Amplified Intelligence; Lee et al. 2025 dataset statistics; creator practice).
- **Measure:** A new visual event inside 3-5 s on the timeline. After publishing: the Studio retention curve should
  have no sharp drop at 3-6 s.

### Rule 17. Push in, or have something move toward the lens, in the first second
- **Rule:** The camera moves toward the subject (at least about 10 % growth in subject size over 1 s), or the subject
  moves toward the lens. A slow push-in can carry the hook line.
- **Mechanism:** Looming motion gets priority; so do sounds that grow louder.
- **Evidence:** A (Franconeri & Simons 2003; Neuhoff / Ghazanfar et al. 2002).
- **Measure:** Compare the subject's bounding-box height at 0 s and 1 s (at least about 1.1x). This must stay within
  the owner's pacing rule: no whips, framings held at least 2.5 s.

### Rule 18. One concrete number or odd detail in the first 5 s
- **Rule:** Use concrete details over general ones: "over a thousand people", "his yellow suitcase", "six to one".
- **Mechanism:** Numbers raised clicks. Precision signals confidence. Odd details are remembered.
- **Evidence:** C (Banerjee & Urminsky working paper; Jerez-Fernandez et al. 2014; the von Restorff effect).
- **Measure:** At least one number or unusual concrete noun in the 0-5 s lines. No vague words ("things", "stuff")
  in the hook.

### Rule 19. No narration over a static picture in 0-5 s
- **Rule:** While the voice talks in the hook, the picture keeps *doing* something (a character acts, the camera
  moves, an object changes).
- **Mechanism:** Motion holds the eye. Talk over a still frame pulls attention apart and feels like a slow start.
- **Evidence:** D (follows from rules 1 and 2; our clown cut 1 was rejected for a static camera).
- **Measure:** For every 0.5 s window in 0-5 s, frame change (`YDIF`) stays above a low floor. By eye: no window
  longer than 1 s where only the mouth moves.

### Rule 20. The ending flows back into frame 0
- **Rule:** The last shot (or line) cuts cleanly into the first, and ideally makes frame 0 mean more on the second
  view.
- **Mechanism:** Replays count as views. Average viewed can pass 100 %. A hook that rewards a re-watch adds real watch
  time.
- **Evidence:** B for the counting rules (YouTube Help; the March 2025 change). C for the effect on ranking (Hoyos).
- **Measure:** Play the last 2 s straight into the first 2 s: no jump in brightness, framing or sound level. After
  publishing: average viewed above 100 % on Shorts under 40 s.

## 11. How to measure a draft before it goes out

**Machine checks on the rendered draft** (run from the folder holding `draft.mp4`):

```bash
# frame-0 brightness (rule 12): look for YAVG; under about 90 means a dim opener (guess threshold)
ffmpeg -hide_banner -i draft.mp4 -vf "select=eq(n\,0),signalstats,metadata=print:key=lavfi.signalstats.YAVG" -frames:v 1 -f null - 2>&1 | grep YAVG

# motion in the first 0.5 s (rules 1, 19): YDIF per frame; frames 1-12 should not be near zero
ffmpeg -hide_banner -i draft.mp4 -t 0.5 -vf "signalstats,metadata=print:key=lavfi.signalstats.YDIF" -f null - 2>&1 | grep YDIF

# first sound (rule 9): the first silence_end time is when sound starts; no silence at 0 means sound from frame 0
ffmpeg -hide_banner -i draft.mp4 -t 1 -af silencedetect=noise=-35dB:d=0.02 -f null - 2>&1 | grep silence

# export frame 0 for the flash test (rule 2)
ffmpeg -hide_banner -i draft.mp4 -frames:v 1 frame0.png
```

**Human micro-tests (5-8 real people beat any simulated panel; our panels are forecasts):**

1. **Flash test** (rules 2 and 6): frame 0 for 0.25 s. "What did you see?"
2. **Muted 1.7-s test** (rules 10 and 11): play 0-1.7 s muted. "What is this about? Keep watching or swipe?"
3. **Question test** (rule 4): stop at 2.5 s. "What do you want to find out?"
4. **Seen-it test** (rule 5): "Have you seen a video about this? Do you know how it ends?"
5. **Feed-sandwich test** (the closest thing to the real feed): put the draft in position 5 of 10 real Shorts on a
   phone (a private playlist or a local vertical player). Let people scroll normally. Note who swipes before 3 s.
   Run the A/B/C hook variants with different people. This is the only test that measures *swiping* rather than
   opinions.

**After publishing:** compare stayed-to-watch with the clown's 68 % (target at least 75 %). Read the first-5-s shape of
the retention curve. Judge average viewed separately; it measures the body, not the hook.

## 12. Limits of this note

- Almost none of the lab work uses phone feeds or cartoons. The direction of each effect is solid; its size on Shorts
  is unknown.
- Platform numbers (Facebook, TikTok, Google, Vidmob, Kantar) are self-published or sponsored, and some hide their
  methods. Creator numbers (Galloway, Hoyos) are informal.
- YouTube does not publish how many seconds count as "stayed", the ranking weights, or any benchmark.
- Our own data is two Shorts. Treat every threshold marked **(guess)** as a starting value to tune with real results.
