Writer's draft, 2026-11-20. Replaces v3 (rejected 1/10). Built in the anti-theft shape: the strange act -> where it came

## A. THE STORY IN TEN PLAIN SENTENCES (as I would tell it to a friend)

1. In Japan the train driver sits alone at the front, and he points at a green light and shouts at it.
2. Tourists film him because it looks like he is arguing with the traffic lights.
3. The story goes that over a hundred years ago a steam-train driver called Hori was losing his eyesight, so he shouted
   every signal to his fireman, and the fireman shouted it back.
4. His boss rode along one day, thought it was genius and made it the rule. He had no idea Hori was just squinting.
5. The finger was added later, and it spread. But does shouting at a light actually do anything?
6. In 1994 Japan's railway lab set a trap: press a button on "go", never on red, then a hundred go lights and one red.
7. Of twelve people who just looked, five pressed anyway (three of them did it again), and of twelve who pointed and
   called, not one did.
8. That's because after a hundred of the same your hand is on autopilot, and pointing and calling adds about a third
   of a second, a pause that beats autopilot. It's like staring right at the stove and still going back to check.
9. So he isn't pointing at nothing. He's pointing his brain at it.
10. New York copied it in 1996, so subway conductors must point at a striped board, and in 2013 two pranksters held
    a sign under it, "Point here if NY is the greatest city in the world", and the conductors had to point.

### The side-by-side read (done before writing a line of narration)

Read next to anti-theft (SCRIPT.md), "Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work" and "The Instinct That Makes You Crash":

- **Opening.** Railroads: "He sawed gaps into a railroad every 39 feet." Ours: a man alone pointing at a light and
  shouting at it. Same kind of hook: a strange act you can picture, with the reason hidden. It is also the title's
  promise, which v3 never showed.
- **The person and the paradox.** Anti-theft: an honest woman who wants to steal. Ours: a driver losing his eyesight whose
  weakness gets copied as genius. This is our "We'd better get ours now": a true, absurd turn told by a real source.
- **The experiment.** Anti-theft gives two signs and two numbers. Ours gives a trap and three numbers (5 of 12, 3 again,
  0 of 12), and the strange act CAUSES the result: pointing is the only difference between five presses and none.
  That is stronger than v3, where pointing caused nothing.
- **The ending.** Anti-theft: "So some of it got stolen twice." Ours: New York's rule gets hijacked by a prank sign.
  It is new in the last 10 s, true and funny.
- **What I changed after this read.** My first ten sentences began with Hori, which put the strange act second. I
  moved the driver to line 1 and the tourists to line 3 so the first 8 s are the act, the call and the doubter. I also
  dropped the 1965 test and the "six times fewer" number, because a second experiment reads like a list. The trap test
  alone carries the proof.
- **Honest gap.** Instinct has a pile of slapstick events (lawn chair, the stump). Ours is less slapstick. The laughs come
  from absurd true facts (the squint, "did it again", the sign), like our own best films, not from physical comedy,
  which the cards cannot do anyway.

## D. THREE ALTERNATIVE FIRST LINES

1. "In Japan, train drivers point at nothing... and shout at it." Echoes the title word for word. Rejected: the picture
   shows a green light, so "nothing" is visibly untrue (FACTS 8: "pointing at nothing" works as a hook only), and the
   punchline in line 22 needs the "nothing" idea to come from the doubters, not from the narrator.
2. "Every day, Japanese train drivers argue with traffic lights." Funny, but it spends the tourist joke in the first
   second, "every" is a claim FACTS warns about, and a narrator calling rail signals "traffic lights" sounds wrong.
3. "He's driving over a thousand people... and he's shouting at a light." TRUE (a 10-car E233 set is rated for 1,582),
   with a stake. Rejected: the thousand people never come back in the story, which is exactly what the benchmark
   criticised in v3 ("1,000 people behind him" never pays off).

**Chosen:** "Alone at the front of a train, he points at a light... and shouts at it." It is a strange act you can
picture in a quarter of a second, the reason is hidden, "alone" makes it stranger (who is he shouting to?), and it is
true in the FACTS wording ("alone at the front"). The narrator's first word lands with frame 0.

  the third "strange act" in a row before the story starts, and the word budget needed the room for the trap test's
  "did it again". It is the FIRST thing to add back if the voice runs under 60 s (put it after line 2, picture: the
  white glove at the timetable card "8:14:30").
- **"About six times fewer mistakes"** (Experiment I, 2.38 vs 0.38 per 100). A second experiment turns the middle into a
  list. The trap test has the better picture and the cause-and-effect.
- **The 1913 date said aloud.** It is on the manual prop in line 10 instead. Said aloud it was a date-and-document
  sentence with no event in it.
- **The fake cab for the training video** (TRUE and funny). It made the lab section longer and slightly undercuts the
  test right when we need the viewer to trust it.
- **The tourist-and-suitcase plot, the boy's copy gags, the lost-and-found.** All invented; the lead dropped them. The
  boy and mum stay as faces in line 11 only.
- **New York's 57 % fall in wrong stops, the prank's view counts, the "dead sexy" sign, Toronto, the Shinkansen safety
