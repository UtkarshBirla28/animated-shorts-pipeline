# Fact read of SCRIPT_v4_draft.md (reader: fact-checker + train fan), 2026-11-20

Checked every narration line and every picture line against FACTS_v4.md (statuses, safe wordings, "do not say"
list). Where the script goes past FACTS I opened sources myself:

- Haga, Akatsuka, Shiroto 1996 (RTRI), full PDF from J-STAGE, Experiment II method, Table 2 and Table 3, read in full.
- ABC News 2013-10-31 (archived copy, full text), Mental Floss 2016 (archived copy), viewing.nyc 2015.
- Search on Japanese semaphore signals (trafficnews.jp, bestcarweb.jp): arm level = stop, arm lowered 45 degrees =
  proceed; semaphores have been used since Meiji.

New facts from the primary paper that FACTS_v4 did not have:
- Trap test (Exp. II) "go" signals were four aspects, not one: proceed (green), reduce speed (green + yellow),
  caution (yellow), restricted (two yellows). Only stop was red.
- Exp. II subjects: 24 RTRI staff, **6 of them women**, aged 21-31. 20 of them had also done Exp. I. Each person was
  tested alone at the same cab mock-up and screen, and a "ready lamp" triggered each try.
- **Table 3: in the trap test, pointing and calling took reactions from 0.812 s to 1.570 s (+0.76 s).** The
  "+0.32 s" (0.751 to 1.074 s) comes from Exp. I, the colour-key test, which the film no longer shows.
- The paper's buzzer sounded when someone did NOT press on a go signal. Nothing sounded on a wrong press.
- Mental Floss: the conductor points "after pulling into the station" and "before we open the doors". ABC: "The
  conductor's point is the sole indicator it is now safe to open the doors."
- ABC: the makers were "Lerner, 25, and his friend, Rose Sacktor, 24", so one man and one woman. They said: "We wanted
  to let them know we see you, we know you're working and we appreciate what you do." The result was "nothing but
  smiles and laughter". viewing.nyc: they "stood underneath the signs" (the boards), so "held a sign under it" is right.

## 2. Pictures: what a railway fan or a New Yorker would call wrong

**Must fix (factually wrong on screen)**
- **23 NYC station: "train doors" in the "what else moves" list.** The doors must stay SHUT until the conductor has
  pointed. Pointing is the go-ahead to open them (ABC, Mental Floss, MTA via AOPA). Doors open only after his point,
  or not at all in this shot. Every New Yorker knows this order.
- **24 NYC platform: "steam from a floor vent".** Steam comes up from street manholes, not on subway platforms. It
  reads as movie New York. Swap it for a train's headlight glow in the tunnel, or a rat on the track bed.
- **24-26 the pranksters: "two young pranksters".** It was one man and one woman: Yosef Lerner (25) and Rose
  Sacktor (24). Draw one of each.
- **5 and 9 steam-era signal: "a wooden signal post".** It must be a SEMAPHORE: a wooden post with an arm. Hori calls
  "clear", so the arm is lowered at 45 degrees (Japanese semaphores: arm level = stop, lowered 45 degrees = proceed).
  No colour lamp lit in daylight. On line 9 the "crisp, clear signal" is the same 45-degree arm.
- **2 platform: "low shot past the green starting signal ... big in the foreground", train coming toward us.** The
  signal faces the oncoming train. From behind it you see the BACK of the signal head, and no green lamp shows. Put
  the camera beside the line, ahead of the signal and angled back, so both the lit face and the train are in frame.
  Or shoot from the train side.
- **9 Hori "squints HARD (eyes as slits, held)".** On a Japanese character, slit eyes read as the old slant-eye
  caricature. That is a real respect risk for a channel watched in Japan. Show the bad eyes another way: brow
  scrunched, head pushed forward, nose almost on the glass, one eye shut, the blurry view out of the window. If the
  narration changes to "eyes were bad", line 9 does not need a squint at all.
- **20 modern cab: "the platform sliding by" while the train waits at red.** The platform is still until the signal
  turns green. Only then does it move.

**Should fix (a fan would notice; staging, not a lie)**
- **14 "the counter flicks 98, 99, 100 in green".** In the real trap the 100 go signals were mixed: green,
  green + yellow, yellow, two yellows. Mixing the aspects is truer and still reads as "go, not red".
- **12-17 the volunteers.** Each person was tested alone at a driver's-cab mock-up with a screen. The cab was a prop
  for JR West's training video. Rows of 24 working at once is a cartoon compression and fine, but put women in
  (6 of 24), and consider showing the cab mock-up: it is truer and funnier than "candy-coloured boards".
- **15-16 "buzzer lamps flash" on the wrong press.** Nothing in the paper sounded on a wrong press. The buzzer was for
  NOT pressing on go. Use a red "X" board, or the researcher's reaction, instead of a machine alarm.
- **1, 20, 22 modern driver.** Add the uniform cap. Japanese drivers wear one with the white gloves.
- **2-3 "departure board flipping / ticking".** Split-flap boards are almost gone in Japan. Modern platforms have LED
  boards.
- **8 the official in "dark suit, bowler hat".** A bowler reads as an English gentleman. A railway-bureau official
  riding a footplate in late Meiji is safer in a dark railway uniform with a peaked cap. (Western suits were worn in
  Meiji, so this is a judgment call, not an error.)
- **4 / 6 the steam cab.** Steam drivers sighted signals through the small round front glasses or by leaning out of
  the side, because the boiler blocks the view ahead. "Leans toward the front window" works if it is the small round
  spectacle glass, not a big windscreen.
- **22 the driver "points down the platform at the starting signal".** The starting signal is ahead, at the end of
  the platform. He points FORWARD along the track. Sweeping a finger down the length of the platform is what
  conductors and station staff do, not the driver.
- **23, 26 the NYC conductor.** MTA navy uniform, no white gloves, no Japanese-style cap, so he does not read as a
  copy of the Tokyo driver. He leans out of a cab window in the MIDDLE of the train (correct as drawn).
- **23 "grey-green tiles".** NYC stations are mostly white or cream tile with coloured mosaic name bands. Pigeons are
  plausible at elevated or open stations and rare deep underground.

**Respect**
- **3 "one tourist doubles over laughing, both hands on his belly"** at a working driver, plus the note "a grown man
  in white gloves scolding a lamp". Line 22 pays it back, so the arc is fair. Still, a grin while filming is enough;
  a belly laugh at a Japanese railwayman tips into mockery. The narration itself is kind.
- **24-26 the prank.** The makers framed it as thanks ("we appreciate what you do") and the conductors smiled. The
  film shows the grin (26), which is good. Do not let any caption call the conductors robots or fools.
- No Japanese words, no "it's the law", no Shinkansen or punctuality claim. The "do not say" list is clean apart from
  the line-10 cut-list risk.
