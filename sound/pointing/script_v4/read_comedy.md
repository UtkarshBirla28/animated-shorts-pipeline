# Comedy read: POINTING v4 draft (reader = comedy)

I read everything as a viewer hears it: the spoken words, plus what the picture shows.

Read in this order:
1. Anti-theft (published, 24.6k views, 91.5 % average watched)
2. Clown v6
3. Three Ricks Clips transcripts: "The Instinct That Makes You Crash", "How One Cart Stopped an Entire Army" and "The Pink Prison Cell Experiment". I also skimmed "Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work" because the draft compares itself to it.
4. Pointing v4, sections B and C

---

## 1. Ranking: the one I'd most want to watch first

1. **The Instinct That Makes You Crash.** It packs the most laughs into the shortest time. Each beat makes the last one worse: a flashing arrow doubles the crashes, the tow-truck guy brings a lawn chair, the sheriff crashes at 15 mph, he chops the tree down, then he hits the stump. The explanation comes at 42 s, and by then I'm hooked. The last line ("Even when it's just a stump") is a callback that's also a joke.
2. **How One Cart Stopped an Entire Army.** The mystery ("what's in the holes?") holds for 50 s. Every beat is a funny thing a person does: the "grating cheese" line, the slap, the human umbrella. Then the payoff lands all at once ("not a single one misses").
3. **The Pink Prison Cell Experiment.** "Barbie's gonna love it in here." The 300 lb inmate loses an arm-wrestle to the skinniest guard. Then comes the reversal: past 15 minutes it does the opposite. The last line actually changes what you thought you'd learned.
4. **Anti-theft.** The best first line of ours: an honest woman wants to steal. "We'd better get ours now" is a real laugh. The "stolen twice" ending ties back to the bait wood, which is why it works. The middle gets a bit lecture-y ("we copy each other"), but it's short.
5. **Clown v6.** The play-along hook is strong, and "Did you see the money?" makes you rewatch it, which none of the others do. "I own a unicycle... and a clown suit" is a laugh. The interview answers in the middle are flat ("Dude, the unicycle guy!"), and "a call pulls your mind toward someone who isn't there" is a lecture line.
6. **Pointing v4.** It has one real laugh ("He never knew Hori was squinting"), one good "no way" (5 of 12 against 0 of 12) and a funny last story (the New York prank sign). But it's three short stories in a row: Hori, then the lab, then New York. Each one starts the clock again, and nothing carries across them the way the stump or the cart does. The opening act is something many viewers have already seen. And the 41-48 s stretch is a classroom.

The gap from 5 to 6 is small. The gap from 4 to 6 is not.

## 3. Dead and weak lines, rewritten

- **3** "Tourists think he's arguing with traffic lights." -> **"Tourists film him like he's yelling at nothing."** This sets up line 22, so it becomes a real callback. If you want a bigger laugh here, try "To tourists, he's a man losing an argument with a lamp."
- **4** "Over a century ago, the story goes, a steam train driver named Hori was losing his eyesight." -> **"The story goes, it started with a steam train driver named Hori. (beat) He was losing his eyesight."** It uses the same safe wording, but the turn gets its own sentence and its own beat.
- **5** "So he shouted every signal to his fireman." -> **"So he shouted every signal... and made his fireman shout it back."** This sets up the "Clear!" echo so it lands as a joke rather than a repeat.
- **10** "The finger came later." -> cut it and keep the glove sticker on the prop board. If a spoken line is needed: **"Years later, someone added the finger."**
- **11** "But does shouting at a light do anything?" -> **"But does yelling at a lamp actually work? In 1994, Japan set a trap."** That's lines 11 and 12 in one, a beat quicker.
- **13** "Press the button on go. Never on red." -> **"Green light? Press the button. Red? Don't."**
- **16** "Three of them did it again." -> **"Ten lights later, another red. Three of them... pressed it again."** It's true (try 111) and now it's a laugh, not a puzzle.
- **18-20** (three lines, 7 s, "autopilot" twice) -> **"After a hundred greens, your hand stops asking your eyes. Pointing and shouting costs a third of a second... just long enough for your brain to catch up."** It's one thought, and "your hand stops asking your eyes" is a picture, not a word from a lesson.
- **21** "Like staring at the stove... then going back to check." -> **"It's why you can stare right at the stove... and still drive back to check it."** Then, for the shareable beat: **"Train drivers would just point at it. 'Stove: off.'"** It's a laugh, and viewers can use it tonight. That's the comment and the share.
- **22** "He's not pointing at nothing. He's pointing his brain at it." -> it can stay once line 3 says "nothing". Otherwise: **"He's not pointing at the light. He's pointing his brain at it."**
- **23** "...must point too: at a striped board." -> **"New York copied it. Subway conductors must point at a zebra board that says 'you stopped in the right spot.'"** Check the board's purpose in FACTS before using that wording.
- **24** "So two pranksters held a sign under it." -> **"Which gave two New Yorkers an idea."** A small hold, then the sign.
- **26** "And the conductors... had to point." -> keep it. Here's an option with a slightly bigger wink that still ends on "point" for the loop: **"Rules are rules. The conductors... had to point."**

## 4. Is the first line a reason to stay?

Only half of one. It describes a strange act, but it's a *known* strange act. What I haven't heard before is that **it began because a man couldn't see**, and that's the anti-theft-grade turn ("an honest woman wants to steal"). Put that inside the first 4 seconds, for example:

> "Japan's train drivers point and shout at every signal... because, the story goes, one of them couldn't see."

or keep the picture of the point and say:

> "He points at a light and shouts at it. The story goes, it started with a driver who couldn't see it."

Check FACTS: "couldn't see" may need the "losing his eyesight" wording. Something like "...with a driver who was going blind" is NOT allowed. Use "...with a driver who was losing his eyesight."

---

## 5. Does the ending give me something new?

Yes, the New York prank is new and funny, and it's the best thing in the last 15 s. But it reads as a **bonus story**, not a twist on what I just learned. Anti-theft's "stolen twice" works because it flips the bait wood back on itself. Here the irony (a rule meant to beat autopilot gets used by a piece of cardboard) is in the writer's notes but not in the spoken words, so viewers won't feel it. Either let "Rules are rules" carry the wink, or tie the ending back to Hori. As it stands, Hori disappears at 23 s and never comes back, and that's the loose thread I'd notice.

## 7. The biggest change

**Make it one story with one turn, not three short stories.** Open on the squint: Japan's famous safety ritual started because a man couldn't see. Then the twist the viewer doesn't expect: *the accident actually works* (the trap test, 5 against 0). Then the stove tip as the everyday payoff, and New York as the last laugh. The spine becomes "a mistake that turned out to be science", and every section moves it forward. Right now each section starts over from zero. Cut the classroom stretch (lines 18-20) down to one picture line.
