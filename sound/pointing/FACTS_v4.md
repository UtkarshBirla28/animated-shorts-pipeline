**H. An independent academic source on the history: Haga et al. 1996 (RTRI paper, section 2; opened).** It does
NOT mention Hori. It says (citing 関東鉄道学園 1970):
- 「信号喚呼は鉄道創業まもないころに「生活の知恵」として自然発生的に生まれたものと言われている」 = "signal-calling
  is said to have arisen naturally, as 'everyday wisdom', soon after the railways began". So calling signals aloud
  is OLDER than Hori; his story is the origin of the two-man call-and-response RULE, not of calling itself.
- 「鉄道省～国鉄が信号喚呼および喚呼応答を規則として初めて義務づけたのは 1947 年と思われる」 = the national railway
  first made calling and call-and-response compulsory nationwide probably in 1947 (the 1913 manual was the Kobe
  bureau's own).
- 「「指差」の方は，昭和の初年頃から東京近郊の乗務員が自発的に信号喚呼に指差しを併用し始めたものが全国に広がっていったと言う」 = pointing: "from around the first years of Showa [1926 on], crews near TOKYO began, on their own, to
  add pointing to signal-calling, and it spread nationwide". 「こちらの方は本社レペルで義務づけられることはなく，1970 年に地方レペルでルール化された。」 = never made compulsory by head office; made a rule at regional level in 1970.
So: calling = from the start; Kobe call-and-response rule = late Meiji / 1913 manual; pointing = crews near Tokyo,
late 1920s; written rules for pointing = 1970. (Kobe is the call-and-response story, Tokyo is the pointing story.)

Verdict on Hori, plainly: the story is real in the sense that it is printed in a 1962 railwayman's book and
repeated by a former driver (2022), Japanese Wikipedia and GIGAZINE. It is not documented anywhere else I could
find (no JR, museum or academic source names him; the RTRI paper's history omits him). It must be told as "the
story goes" / "according to an old railway book".

## 2. THE 1994 EXPERIMENT (Railway Technical Research Institute)

### 2.1 The primary paper (opened, full text, 8 pages)

Haga Shigeru, Akatsuka Hajime, Shiroto Hiroaki (all at the Railway Technical Research Institute, RTRI),
「指差呼称」のエラー防止効果の室内実験による検証 / "Laboratory experiments for verifying the effectiveness of
'finger-pointing and call' as a practical tool of human error prevention", 産業・組織心理学研究 (Japanese
Association of Industrial/Organizational Psychology Journal) vol. 9 no. 2, 1996, pp. 107-114.
J-STAGE PDF: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jaiop/9/2/9_107/_pdf/-char/ja  (DOI 10.32222/jaiop.9.2_107)

Why "1994" and "1996" both appear: the paper says 「1994 年には鉄道総研も，JR 西日本から社員教育用ビデオ教材のために新しい実験デークを求められた。」 = "In 1994 RTRI was asked by JR West for new experimental data for a staff-training
VIDEO." It was presented at a 1995 conference and published in 1996. So "a 1994 test" (labour ministry, Flight Safety
Australia, Japan Times) and "a 1996 study" (Atlas Obscura) are the SAME experiment. Safest: "In 1994".
(Text-extraction note: the PDF is an OCR scan; some kanji in my quotes come out garbled, e.g. デーク = データ,
ビデオ shown as ピデオ. The numbers are clean.)

**The task (Experiment I), exact:**
- Who: 「鉄道総研職員 24 人（うち，女性 4 人）が実験に参加した。年齢は 21 歳から 31 歳の間であった。」 = 24 RTRI staff
  (4 women), aged 21 to 31. NOT train drivers.
- Where: 「被験者は，運転台モックアッブの前に着座し」 = seated in front of a mock-up of a driver's cab, 5 keys at hand,
  a 17-inch CRT screen 2.4 m away.
- What: 「ディスブレイに描画した鉄道信号機様の図形（黒色の縦長梢円）の中央に直径 21mm の色のついた円（以下「信号」と呼ぶ）が 2 秒間隔でつぎつぎに表示される。信号の色は赤，白，黄，緑，青のいずれかである。被験者に課された作業は，信号の色に対応した反応キーをできるだけ早く正確に押すことである。」 = a coloured circle inside
  a drawing of a railway signal appeared every 2 seconds: red, white, yellow, green or blue; the job was to press the
  key of that colour as fast and as accurately as possible.
- Four ways, each person did all four (order varied): no pointing or calling; pointing only; calling the colour only;
  pointing and calling. 105 tries each (first 5 not counted).
- Honest caveat in the paper: 「なお，この作業課題は鉄逍における現実の作業を実験室内にシミュレートしたものではない。運転台モックアップや鉄迫信号に擬した刺激を用いたのは，鉄道会社のピデオ教材の中でこの実験が紹介される際の説得力を砧める効果をねらったものである。」 = "This task does not simulate real railway work. The cab mock-up and
  signal-like stimuli were used to make it more persuasive when shown in the railway company's training video."
  (True "no way" detail: the cab was set dressing for a training film.)

**The numbers (Table 1, mean wrong presses per person per 100 tries):**

| condition | mean errors per 100 | people (of 24) with at least one error |
|---|---|---|
| no pointing, no calling | **2.375** | 15 (62.5 %) |
| pointing only | **0.750** | 12 (50.0 %) |
| calling only | **1.000** | 10 (41.7 %) |
| pointing and calling | **0.375** | 5 (20.8 %) |

Statistics in the paper: only "pointing only" and "pointing and calling" were significantly lower than "neither"
(Dunnett, p < .01). "Calling only" was NOT shown to differ significantly on its own.

**Labour ministry page (opened, Shift_JIS decoded), https://anzeninfo.mhlw.go.jp/yougo/yougo72_1.html :**
「1994年、財団法人（現、公益財団法人）鉄道総合技術研究所により、効果検定実験が行われました。同実験によれば、「指差しと呼称を、共に行わなかった」場合の操作ボタンの押し間違いの発生率が2.38%であったのに対し、「呼称のみ行った」場合の押し間違いの発生率は1.0%、「指差しだけ行った」場合の押し間違いの発生率は0.75%でした。一方、指差しと呼称を「共に行った場合」の押し間違いの発生率は0.38%となり、…約6分の1という結果でした。」
= 1994, RTRI; wrong button presses 2.38 % (neither), 1.0 % (calling only), 0.75 % (pointing only), 0.38 % (both):
"about one-sixth". Same page: 「指差呼称だけでヒューマンエラーの根絶を実現することはできませんが」 = "pointing and
calling alone cannot wipe out human error".

**Second independent English source:** Flight Safety Australia 2018 (opened): "In 1994, the Japanese Railway Technical
Research Institute assessed pointing and calling in an experiment that asked volunteers to complete a simple, but
variable, task. When no special steps were taken to prevent errors, the volunteers made 2.38 errors per 100 actions.
... the greatest reduction in error to 0.38 errors per 100 actions happened when the volunteers both pointed and
called their actions. This combination reduced mistakes by almost 85 per cent."

**The ratio:** 2.375 / 0.375 = 6.33 ("about six times fewer": TRUE). Cut = (2.375 - 0.375) / 2.375 = 84.2 %.
"Almost 85 %" is fine; "85 %" flat is a round-up; "more than 80 %" is exact-safe.

### 2.2 Experiment II (the "trap" test): stronger and more vivid, same paper

「第 101 試行目に初めて停止現示を出したところ，指差呼称をしてから反応キーを押す条件では 1 人もキーを押さなかったのに対し，指差呼称をしない条件では 12 人中 5 人が知らせ灯点灯に「つり込まれて」反応してしまった。」
= People watched a small "ready" lamp; when it lit, they looked up at a signal and pressed the key unless the signal
was RED (stop). For 100 tries it was never red. On try 101 it was red. Of the 12 who pointed and called first, NONE
pressed. Of the 12 who did not, 5 pressed anyway, "pulled in" by habit. 「この 5 人のうち 3 人は，9 試行を間にはさんだ後の第 111 試行において再びエラー反応をおかした。」 = 3 of those 5 did it AGAIN on try 111.
English abstract of the paper: "None of 12 subjects who reacted to the lamp after FPAC committed an error, while 5
subjects out of 12 under the no-FPAC condition pressed the key on the red light."
The paper says this models a real accident type, the "wrong departure" (誤出発事故): a driver starts against a red
signal because the departure cue came. (Small groups, 12 vs 12; 0/12 vs 5/12 is significant, Fisher p < .05.)

### 2.3 The cost (true; a train fan may know it)

Table 3: pointing and calling made reactions SLOWER. Exp. I mean 0.751 s (neither) vs 1.074 s (both); Exp. II
0.812 s vs 1.570 s. Paper: 「反応時間は指差呼称をすることにより増大するので，秒単位の迅速さが求められる作業には不適であることは言うまでもない。」 = "Since it increases reaction time, it is of course unsuited to work needing
split-second speed." It also warns that pointing must never replace better equipment, and that forcing it too often
turns it into empty ritual (形骸化).

### 2.4 The older study (1965): do not mix them up

Kiyomiya Eiichi (清宮栄一) et al., JNR labour-science institute, 1965 (『鉄道労働科学』No. 17, 289-295; summarised in
Haga 1996 and ja.wiki): five lamps lit at random every 1.5 s; errors: neither 2.85 %, pointing 1.50 %, calling 1.25 %,
both 0.80 %. The 2.38 / 0.38 numbers are the 1994 re-run, NOT 1965.

## 3. WHY IT WORKS (as researchers put it)

**A. Haga et al. 1996 (primary), quoting the classic explanation (Iiyama 1980):**
「（1）指差により自己を租極的に対象に近づけるため，刺激を正確かつ鮮明に網膜に伝える，（2）名称を思い出して言うという思考を必要とするため，注意力をより長く，強く対象に集中させ，短期記憶の形成を助ける，（3）視知覚，聴党，筋肉知覚などを動貝して対象を認知することにより認知の正確度が高まる，（4）アゴや手や腕の筋肉運動（特に発声に伴う咬筋の運動）が刺激となって大脳の活動レペルが上がるため」
= (1) pointing brings you actively toward the target so the image reaches the eye sharply; (2) recalling and saying
the name holds attention on the target longer and harder and helps short-term memory; (3) using sight, hearing and
muscle sense together makes recognition more accurate; (4) the jaw, hand and arm movement (especially the jaw muscle
when speaking) raises the brain's activity level.
The authors' OWN view: 「今回の実験で指差呼称がエラーを予防した最大の要因は，刺激の知覚と反応の間に別の動作とそれに伴うクイムラグが挿入されたことによる，焦燥反応や習恨的動作の抑制であると思われる。」 = "the main reason it prevented
errors here seems to be that inserting another action, and its time lag, between seeing and reacting held back hasty
and habitual responses." And 「これらの仮説について否定も肯定もできない」 = they could neither confirm nor deny the
classic hypotheses.

**B. Labour ministry page:** 「対象を指で差し、声に出して確認する行動によって、意識レベルを「フェーズⅢ（脳が活発に動き、思考が前向きな状態）」に上げ、緊張感、集中力を高める効果をねらった行為とされています。」 = "it is said to aim to
raise your level of consciousness to 'Phase III' (the brain working actively), raising alertness and focus". (Phase
theory = Hashimoto Kunie's (橋本邦衛) model: a framework, not a measurement.)

**C. Japanese Wikipedia** gives the four steps: 「目で見て / 腕を伸ばし指で指して / 口を開き声に出して「○○○、ヨシ!」/ 耳で自分の声を聞く」 = look with the eyes; stretch the arm and point; open the mouth and say "..., yoshi!"; hear
your own voice.

**D. Atlas Obscura** (quoting Japan's National Institute of Occupational Safety and Health): "raising the
consciousness levels of workers".

**E. Brain findings.**
- MHLW page cites a 2010 Hiroshima University (Graduate School of Health Sciences) near-infrared (fNIRS) study of
  nurses' drug checks: 「『指差し呼称』法のほうが、『黙読』法、『指差し』法、『呼称』法よりも前頭葉におけるHV（前頭葉における血中酸素化ヘモグロビン変化量）が多かった」 = pointing-and-calling gave MORE oxygenated-blood change in the
  frontal lobe than silent reading, pointing alone, or calling alone. Verdict: TRUE as "one small study found more
  front-of-brain activity". It is MORE activity, not less. Small nursing task; do not overstate.
- Flight Safety Australia quotes CASA human-factors specialist Robert Forsterlee: "Your brain is more active when you
  point and speak than when you just think it." Expert opinion, not a study.
- The best "stops autopilot / inhibition" evidence is Haga's Experiment II (2.2). Solid enough for "it stops you
  acting on autopilot", with the small-sample caveat kept off-screen.

**Plainest accurate wording (pick one):**
- "Pointing and saying it forces a tiny pause between seeing and doing. That pause beats autopilot." (= the RTRI
  authors' own explanation.)
- "Eyes, arm, mouth and ears all check the same thing." (= the standard Japanese teaching.)
- AVOID: "doubles your brain power", "rewires your brain", "uses both halves of the brain", "proven by brain
  scans", "scientists don't know why it works".

## 4. WHAT DRIVERS ACTUALLY DO TODAY

**What they point at.**
- Norimono News (乗りものニュース) editorial, 2023-06-15, https://trafficnews.jp/post/126406 (opened):
  「運転士の指差し喚呼の対象は、窓の外の信号機や速度標識から、車内の表示灯、持参の運転時刻表までさまざまです」
  = "A driver's pointing-and-calling targets range from signals and speed signs outside the window to indicator
  lamps in the cab and the running timetable the driver carries." Departure examples: 「戸締め・点（ドアが閉まった合図灯が点灯したのを確認）」 = "Doors shut: lit!" (checking that the all-doors-closed lamp is lit), and 「発車・14時37分45秒定時」 = "Departure: 14:37:45, on time" (checking the timetable). Timetable to the SECOND is real.
- Nippon.com (Nishiue, 2026): drivers point at "signals, the speedometer, track conditions"; a driver's call
  "ATS [automatic train stop] good, radio good..."; a conductor "uses pointing and calling to confirm door opening
  and closing", and confirms the departure time before giving the departure signal.
- Atlas Obscura 2017: "the speedometer will be physically pointed at, with a call of 'speed check, 80'". Station
  staff "point down the track and sweep their arm along the length of the platform -- eyes following the hand --
  before declaring all clear", and again as the train leaves, so no bags or passengers are caught in the doors.
- Haga 1996 (intro): station staff trace the track from left to right with a finger and say 「よし！」 ("Yoshi!" =
  "OK / good") when nothing has fallen on it.
- Japanese Wikipedia: JR East conductor on arrival checks the stop mark: 「○両停止位置よし!」 = "[N]-car stop
  position, OK!" before opening the doors; JR Central: 「○両停止位置、オーライ！」 then, after the doors open,
  「点灯、オーライ！」 ("lamps lit, all right!"). Crossing the track: 「右ヨシ!、左ヨシ!、前ヨシ!」.
- Labour ministry: the standard gesture: point with the right index finger while naming the item, bring the hand
  back to the right ear asking "is it really right?", then swing it down at the target saying 「ヨシっ！」.

**The famous call, "Shuppatsu shinkō!" (出発進行) -- the brief's gloss "departure, proceed" is LOOSE.**
- Norimono News 2019-07-02 (草町義和), https://trafficnews.jp/post/86595/ (opened):
  「「出発進行」のうち「出発」は、「出発信号機」のことです。」 = "The 'shuppatsu' in shuppatsu shinkō means the
  STARTING SIGNAL (the signal at the end of the platform)." 「「進行」は、「信号機がある場所の先の区間に進んでもよい」という意味です。」 = "'Shinkō' means 'you may proceed into the section beyond the signal' (the green aspect)."
  Other calls: 「出発停止」「出発注意」 ("starting signal: stop / caution").
- Norimono News 2026-05-06 (鈴木 哲), https://trafficnews.jp/post/658835/2 (opened): it is a phrase 「「出発信号機」が、「進行信号（緑色＝先の区間に進んでよい条件）」を示していることを確認するための言い回し」, and the 進行 does not mean
  "let's set off".
- Japanese Wikipedia (same): 「出発信号機が進行信号を現示していること」.
- Diamond (Nishiue): the example call is 「出発進行！」.
- SAFE GLOSS: "Shuppatsu shinkō! -- starting signal, clear!" or "Signal: green, go." NOT "Let's go!" and NOT a
  greeting to passengers.

**Is it required?**
- Haga 1996: JR East's operating standard (May 1994) says 「1 所定の確認地点で喚呼する」「2 正しく指差確認する」 =
  "1. call out at the set checking points; 2. point and confirm correctly". JNR regional rule of 1970: 「信号の喚呼は，指差して行うものとする」 = "signals shall be called while pointing".
- Nippon.com: 1970 JNR regulations required pointing when calling signals.
- 日本民営鉄道協会 (Association of Japanese Private Railways) glossary, https://www.mintetsu.or.jp/knowledge/term/16373.html
  (opened): 「民営鉄道では駅務、乗務、または鉄道施設の保守・管理を担当する者に徹底的に訓練し、身につけさせ」 = private
  railways "train [station, crew and maintenance staff] thoroughly until it is second nature".
- Verdict: TRUE WITH WORDING: "It's in the rulebook" / "staff are drilled in it" on Japanese railways. DO NOT say
  "it's the law in Japan" (no law found; these are company/JNR rules) or "every railway does it the same way"
  (Diamond: words and moves 「それぞれ少しずつ異なります」, differ a little by company; Meitetsu calls it 指差称呼).

**Who does it:** drivers, conductors, station staff, track workers (all sources above). TRUE.

**How often:** no source gives a count per trip or per day for a Japanese driver. UNVERIFIED: do not give a number.
(The only per-day figure found is Canadian: GO Transit crew "work and operate 60 to 70 stops a day", Metrolinx.)

**Caveat a train fan knows:** on lines with in-cab signalling (ATC), many outdoor signals are gone, so there is
less to point at outside: Norimono News 2023: ATC lines show speed limits in the cab and outdoor pointing is
becoming less common. Do not say "every driver shouts 'shuppatsu shinkō' at every station".

Safe sentences:
- "Drivers point at the signals, the speed, the clock. Conductors point at the platform and the doors."
- "Station staff sweep a finger down the platform before the train goes."
- "'Shuppatsu shinkō!' Starting signal: green. Go."
- "Every one of these is a rule, not a show."

## 5. NEW YORK: the zebra board

**What and why.**
- Mental Floss (opened), quoting an MTA conductor's 2011 Reddit AMA: "They don't trust us to just look, so
  required procedure is to point to it at every station before we open the doors." and "The absolute biggest
  violation a conductor can make is opening the doors where there isn't a platform." The board, "a black-and-white
  striped bar of wood", when "lined up perfectly with the conductor's window, that tells them it's safe to open the
  doors."
- ABC News 2013 (opened): "Each NYC subway conductor must point at a black-and-white striped conductor's indication
  board" after the train reaches the platform.
- Atlas Obscura 2017: "conductors point to a fixed black-and-white 'zebra board' to confirm a stopped train is
  correctly located along the platform."

**Since when: 1996 (the "1966" is a typo).**
- AOPA Flight Training magazine, Jan 2021, https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2021/january/flight-training-magazine/on-point
  (opened), quoting the MTA: "it wasn't until 'September of 1996 when conductors were first required to physically
  acknowledge the 'zebra boards,' by pointing to them before opening the doors. This was done to create an
  additional level of safety. By pointing at the board, the conductor acknowledges that the train is stopped at the
  proper spot on the platform.'" and "While there is some fogginess over just who made it a requirement, there is
  absolutely no argument over where it originated -- Japan."
- English Wikipedia (citing MTA, 12 Nov 2013, "Subway Conductors Point the Way to Safety"): "implemented in 1996".
- Atlas Obscura: "since 1996 after then Chief Transportation Officer Nathaniel Ford was fascinated by the
  point-and-call system during a business trip to Japan"; MTA spokeswoman Amanda Kwan: "within two years of
  implementation, incidents of incorrectly berthed subways fell 57 percent." (Flight Safety Australia repeats the
  57 % and 1996.)
- Mental Floss: "it wasn't until 1996 that the MTA began requiring conductors to point at them" (boards themselves
  date from "around World War I").
- 1966 appears only in Untapped Cities ("In September 1966 ...", "According to the MTA website") and 6sqft (copying
  it). Both say "September", like the MTA's "September of 1996", so 1966 is a one-digit slip. Also a NYC executive
  copying Japan makes sense in the 1990s, not 1966.
- Verdict: "since 1996" TRUE. "1966" FALSE.

**The reason "doors opened in tunnels".**
- English Wikipedia: "implemented in 1996 after a series of incidents where doors opened in the tunnel" (cited to
  the MTA 2013 page, which I could not open). The MTA words quoted by AOPA give the reason only as "an additional
  level of safety" and "stopped at the proper spot". Atlas Obscura: "incorrectly berthed" trains.
- Verdict: "to make sure the whole train is at the platform before the doors open" TRUE. "After doors opened in a
  tunnel" UNVERIFIED (one secondary source): avoid, or say "so doors never open where there's no platform".

**Important difference:** New York conductors POINT but do not CALL. Atlas Obscura: "a modified point-only
system". And it is the CONDUCTOR (who rides mid-train and works the doors), not the driver ("train operator").

Safe sentences:
- "In New York, subway conductors must point at a black-and-white striped board at every station before they open
  the doors."
- "New York copied it from Japan in 1996."
- "Within two years, the MTA says, trains stopping in the wrong place fell by more than half." (57 %, MTA
  spokeswoman via Atlas Obscura; say "the MTA says".)
- AVOID: "since 1966", "New York drivers point and shout", "after a train opened its doors in a tunnel" (as fact).

## (a) THE 12 MOST USABLE FACTS (ranked: surprise x solidity)

1. **The trap test.** In RTRI's 1994 test, after 100 "go" signals came one red: 5 of 12 people who just looked
   pressed anyway; of 12 who pointed and called, not one. (Haga 1996, primary.) Surprise high, solid.
2. **Six times fewer mistakes.** Coloured-light button test, 1994: 2.38 wrong per 100 just looking, 0.38 per 100
   pointing and calling. (Haga 1996 + labour ministry + Flight Safety Australia.)
3. **New York copied it.** Since 1996 NYC subway conductors must point at a black-and-white striped board at every
   station before opening the doors. (MTA via AOPA; Mental Floss; en.wiki.)
4. **The prank.** In 2013 two New Yorkers held "Point here if..." signs under that board, so conductors had to
   point at them ("Point here if NY is the greatest city in the world"). (ABC News, YouTube, Daily Dot.)
5. **The Hori story (told as a story).** An old railway book says a driver named Hori, whose eyes were failing,
   kept checking signals out loud with his fireman; an official riding along didn't know about his eyes, thought
   it was brilliant, and made it the rule. (Imamura 1962 via Diamond 2022, ja.wiki, GIGAZINE.) Surprise very high;
   solidity = one source chain, so "the story goes".
6. **The shout came first.** Calling out signals is older than pointing; it was in a 1913 crew manual; the
   finger was added in the late 1920s, by crews near Tokyo, on their own. (Haga 1996, ja.wiki, nippon.com.)
7. **It makes you slower, on purpose.** Pointing and calling added about a third of a second per reaction; the
   researchers think that pause is what stops autopilot. (Haga 1996.)
8. **"Shuppatsu shinkō" isn't "let's go".** It means "starting signal: green". (Norimono News x2, ja.wiki.)
9. **The 57 %.** The MTA says wrongly-stopped trains fell 57 % within two years of the pointing rule. (Atlas
   Obscura, MTA spokeswoman; one chain.)
10. **Timed to the second.** A driver's departure call can be "Departure: 14:37:45, on time" while pointing at
    the timetable. (Norimono News 2023.)
11. **The lab cab was a prop.** The 1994 test's driver's-cab mock-up was there to look convincing in JR West's
    training video; the authors say the task was not real driving. (Haga 1996.)
12. **Toronto, 2021.** GO Transit adopted "Point and Call -- Shisa Kanko" in March 2021: "clear right, clear
    left". (Metrolinx.) Also: the Tokaido Shinkansen reports no passenger killed or injured on board in an
    accident since 1964 (JR Central) -- true but NOT caused by pointing, so keep it separate if used.

## (b) TEMPTING BUT FALSE OR UNPROVABLE LINES -- DO NOT SAY

- "Hori went blind" / "a blind driver invented it" (sources: eyes "got bad", "failing").
- "He hid his blindness / his secret" (sourced: the OFFICIAL did not notice; Hori hiding it is not stated).
- "Yasoichi Hori" as a firm name (reading of 八十吉 unknown; probably Yasokichi). Say "a driver named Hori".
- "Hori invented pointing" / "it began with a finger" (his story is the call-and-response; pointing came ~15+ years later, near Tokyo).
- "In 1913 Japan made it the law" (it was one bureau's crew manual; nationwide call rule ~1947; pointing rules 1970, regional).
- "It's the law in Japan" / "every railway in the world does it" (company rules; rare outside East Asia).
- "The 1994 test used train drivers" (24 RTRI staff, aged 21-31).
- "Pointing and calling cuts mistakes by 85 %" as an exact number (84 %; say "almost 85 %" or "six times fewer").
- "Saying it out loud alone halves your mistakes" (calling-only was not significant).
- "It makes you faster" (it makes you slower).
- "Brain scans prove it" / "rewires your brain" / "uses both sides of your brain" (one small fNIRS nursing study; more activity, not proof).
- "Shuppatsu shinkō means 'Let's depart!'".
- "New York has done it since 1966" (1996).
- "New York started it after doors opened in a tunnel" (one secondary source; not in the MTA words I could read).
- "New York drivers point and shout" (conductors; point only).
- Any sign text in the 2013 prank beyond the four ABC News listed.
- "The MTA banned the prank" or "conductors were punished" (no source).
- "Pointing is why Japanese trains are on time" / "why the Shinkansen never crashed".
- "Japan's trains carry 12 billion people a year" (unsourced figure).
- "Drivers point at least N times a trip" (no count found).
- "It saved [specific] train / prevented [specific] crash" (no sourced case).
- "The driver is the only crew member" (a conductor usually rides at the back; say "alone at the front").
