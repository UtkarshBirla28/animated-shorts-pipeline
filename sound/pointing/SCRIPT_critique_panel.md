# Viewer panel critique: "Why Japanese Train Drivers Point at Nothing" (SCRIPT v1)

Simulated by one reviewer, 2026-11-19. This is a forecast from judgment, not data. It is calibrated on our two real
results (anti-theft: 24.6k views, 91.5 % average viewed; clown: 1.4k views, 68 % stayed to watch) and on the reference
channel patterns in `research_notes/ricksclips/SCRIPT_REPORT.md`. I watched the script "in my head" as planned: bean-head
2D cartoon characters, a warm game-engine-lit 3D world, a fast moving camera, word-by-word captions, narrator plus
character voices, sound effects and music. Runtime assumed: 64 s.

Facts re-checked for this review: Wikipedia "Pointing and calling" (origin in the steam era, pointing "added a few
decades later"; NYC 1996 "after a series of incidents where doors opened in the tunnel"; RTRI 1994 "almost 85 percent"
on "a simple task"; "not common outside of Asia"); Japan's government site and Wikipedia "Automatic train control"
(the Shinkansen has **no lineside signals**: at its speed the driver cannot read wayside signals, so speed limits are
shown in the cab by ATC); Nippon.com (RTRI: error rate falls to about one-sixth); E235 series (Yamanote 11-car set,
rated about 1,628 passengers).

## 0. Verdict in five lines

- **Better than the clown, weaker than anti-theft.** Predicted stayed to watch **about 74 %** (range 70-78) vs the
  clown's 68 %. Predicted average viewed **about 78 %** (range 70-85) vs anti-theft's 91.5 %.
- **The first 0.3 s is the best cold open we have planned** (a snapping white glove plus a shout). The problem starts at
  1.4 s: the topic is well known, and the script answers the "why" in spirit by 10 s ("a thousand people behind him" =
  it's for safety). After that, the only open question is "how much does it help?", and the answer (85 %) is the most
  repeated fact about this topic.
- **The doubter is planted but does not drive the story.** He laughs at 10 s, vanishes for 30 s, and his comeuppance
  (the suitcase) is a slapstick accident that is not caused by him refusing to point.
- **One factual error a train fan will catch in the first second:** a bullet train has no green lineside signal. The
  Shinkansen uses in-cab signals. "Signal... GREEN!" plus "signal lamp macro" plus "bullet train" is wrong.
- **Easy fix, big gain:** open on the doubter (seen through his own phone) with a promise that his laugh will cost him,
  switch to a packed Tokyo train (where the signal really is a tiny dot that looks like "nothing"), let the tourist set
  up New York, and end with him pointing at his departing suitcase. Predicted with the fixes: stayed **about 80 %**,
  average viewed **about 88 %**.

Why the stayed-to-watch is not higher than 74 %: our panel has one "seen it" viewer, but in the real feed the
"Japanese pointing" topic has been viral for years, so a larger share of fact-savvy viewers recognise it from the
hook line alone. The glove snap buys the first second; the hook line at 1.4 s is where recognition happens.

Why the average viewed is well below anti-theft: anti-theft had a counter-intuitive result (a warning made people

the hero and the doubter for history and New York, and 22 s follow the answer (the script's own target is 14 s).

Comments: likely **higher per view than both** (train fans correcting, people confessing what they check twice,
Japanese viewers adding "we do this at home"). Good for reach, but the train-fan correction is a credibility cost
for a channel that promises true stories.

### 3.2 The 3 biggest swipe risks
1. **1.4-10 s, recognition plus an early answer.** "This man shouts at nobody... and points at nothing." makes the
   topic recognisable ("seen it"), and the shout one second earlier ("Signal... GREEN!") already says he IS pointing
   at something. Then "He's not crazy... over a thousand people behind him" (4.6 s) tells everyone it is a safety
   habit. From 10 s the only open question is "how much does it help?". Nothing in the first 10 s is a question the
   viewer cannot answer from memory. (Dan, and the Tyler type later.)
2. **18.6-39.6 s, the 21-second detour.** "It started over a hundred years ago, on steam trains." (18.6 s) is a
   documentary sentence, and "Outside Asia, few railways bother." (27.0 s) is a second one. Hero and doubter are both
   off screen for 21 s; New York is a new place with new words ("conductors must point at a striped board at every
   stop", 33.4 s). This is where the 15-year-old and the half-English viewer leave.
3. **39.6-64 s, a weak loop line and a long tail.** "But does it actually work?" (39.6 s) is a yes/no question
   everyone answers "yes". The answer (41.8 s) is the single most repeated fact on this topic. Then 22 s of why,
   stove lesson and payoff. The suitcase payoff needs the viewer to notice, in about 4 s, that the bag was inside the
   train, which v1 never shows clearly. Anti-theft lost its biggest share of viewers in the same place (after the
   result).

### 3.3 The 3 strongest moments
1. **0.0-0.3 s, the glove snap with the shout.** Motion and sound in the first 0.3 s, a strange act, a signature
   object (the white glove). Our best first frame so far. Keep it.
2. **12.4-18.6 s, the call-and-response montage.** "Signal, green!" / "Speed, check!" / "Doors, closed!" is short,
   rhythmic, easy for non-native viewers, satisfying like an ASMR checklist, and it plants the payoff line.
3. **24-31 s, the two visual gags.** The fireman yelling "CLEAR!" without looking up, then pointing with the shovel so
   coal flies off; and the New York commuter looking out at a black tunnel wall, checking his ticket, looking back.
   Both work with the sound off and without English.
   (Honourable mentions: the fly-back through the train at 4.6 s; the jars at 41.8 s; the stove line at 54 s, which
   is the most share-driving line in the script.)

## 5. Concrete fixes (line and picture changes)

1. **Cold open: put the doubter and a promise in the first 3 s** (full comparison in section 6). Frame 0 is the same
   glove snap, but seen through the tourist's phone screen (record dot, vertical frame inside the frame). Lines:
   D "Signal... GREEN!" / T (laughing) "Bro's pointing at NOTHING!" / N "That laugh... is about to cost him."
   This turns a fact question everyone knows ("why point?") into a story question nobody knows ("what will it cost
   him?") and holds it to the last second.
2. **Fix the train (red flag 1)** and make "nothing" literally true: a packed Tokyo train, the signal a tiny green dot
   far down the line. Stakes line: N "Because behind this man... are over a thousand people." (fly-back through the
   packed cars: the sleeping office worker, the grandma with an ekiben, the kid at the cab glass copying the point).
3. **Kill the documentary sentence at 18.6 s.** Replace "It started over a hundred years ago, on steam trains." with
   N "A hundred years ago, it was even louder." (hard cut to the roaring steam cab), then N "On steam trains, the
   driver and the fireman yelled every signal to each other." Same fact, but it opens like a page turn.
4. **Give New York to the doubter.** Replace "Outside Asia, few railways bother. Then, in New York..." with
   T (at a station, hopping off the train with his phone up): "Nobody does that back home!"
   N "Funny. New York points too. After its subway doors started opening... in tunnels."
   The doubter is proven wrong once in the middle (a sting at about 26 s), stays on screen, and the line is truer.
   Shorten the rule for non-native viewers: N "Now conductors must point at a striped board. No board? No doors."
5. **Swap the loop line.** "But does it actually work?" -> N "So... who's the silly one?" (the driver points; the
   tourist points back, mocking). It is a contest, not a yes/no question, and it plants the word "silly" that the
   last line pays off ("You'll feel silly... but not as silly as him.").
6. **Make the payoff clear and caused by HIM not checking.** Plant at about 24 s: he rolls the yellow suitcase into the
   doorway and hops out "for one shot" (1 s, clearly visible). Payoff: platform staff points, "Doors, closed!"; the
   doors close; the train glides away with the yellow suitcase in the window; he lowers his phone... and slowly
   points at it. N "...but not as silly as him." End on "him" with his finger out, which match-cuts into the glove
   point at frame 0 (a cleaner loop than v1's platform-to-cab jump).
7. **Shorten the tail to about 15 s after the answer.** Why: N "You see, your eyes can look... without seeing. The
   finger makes your brain check." Try it: N "So tonight, point at your stove. Say 'off.' Out loud. You'll feel
   silly..." (drop "really" and "actually"; they are filler).
8. **Captions for muted and non-native viewers.** Digits on screen ("1,000", "100 years", "85 %"); shouted words in
   caps; no more than three words per caption chunk in the New York part; frame 0 must read as "a man pointing hard in
   a speeding cab" at thumbnail size (face, glove and window in one frame), with the glove macro at 0.3 s, not frame 0.
9. **Title option** that does not trigger "seen it": "The Tourist Who Laughed at a Train Driver" (backup to test).
   The v1 title is fine for search, but it announces the well-known topic.
10. **Pinned comment:** keep the stove question; it matches what Sophie, Mike and Yuki would write anyway.

## 6. Cold open: v1 vs two alternatives

**v1 (as written).** Glove snap, D "Signal... GREEN!", N "This man shouts at nobody... and points at nothing.",
N "He's not crazy. He's driving a bullet train..."

**A. Doubter first, through his phone (recommended).** Frame 0: the tourist's phone screen (record dot) at the cab
glass; the glove snaps. D "Signal... GREEN!" / T "Bro's pointing at NOTHING!" / N "That laugh... is about to cost
him." / N "Because behind this man... are over a thousand people."
*Variant A2:* the first shout in real Japanese ("Shuppatsu... SHINKOU!"), caption "SIGNAL... GO!". Stranger to 95 % of
viewers and authentic for Japanese ones, but only with a native-quality voice; otherwise stay with A.

**B. "You" first (relatable).** Frame 0: POV, your hand on the front door at night, you turn back. N "You've gone
back to check the stove. Twice." Whip-pan into the cab: glove snap, D "Signal... GREEN!" N "Train drivers in Japan
fixed that problem a hundred years ago... with one finger."

| (1-10) | v1 | A (doubter + promise) | B (you + stove) |
|---|---|---|---|
| frame-0 stopping power | 9 | 9 (same snap, plus a phone frame that looks feed-native) | 5 (a dark doorway, slow) |
| works with the sound off | 7 | 8 (laugh face + caps caption) | 7 |
| open question a "seen it" viewer cannot answer | 3 ("why point?" is known) | **9** ("what will it cost him?") | 5 ("how does a finger fix my stove?", answer half-given) |
| conflict in the first 3 s | 2 (none until 10 s) | **9** | 3 |
| comments / shares | 6 | 7 | **9** (everyone checks something twice) |
| honesty risk | 6 (the Shinkansen signal) | 9 | 8 |
| fit with the ending and the loop | 6 | **10** (laugh at the start, his point at the end, loops into the glove) | 7 (the stove pays off, the tourist is not set up) |
| **total (of 70)** | **39** | **61** | **44** |

Panel check for A: Dan now stays past 3 s (he does not know what happens to the tourist) and probably to about 25 s
or the end; Tyler stays to the end to see the tourist "get got"; Lucas follows a face he recognises through the
middle. That is where the predicted lift (74 % -> about 80 % stayed, 78 % -> about 88 % viewed) comes from.
