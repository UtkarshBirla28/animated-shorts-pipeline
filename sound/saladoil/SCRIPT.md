## Facts (top of SCRIPT.md), all checked
- **The trader:** Tino De Angelis, Allied Crude Vegetable Oil Refining Corp., Bayonne, New Jersey. He traded soybean and cottonseed oil. [W-Tino] [W-Salad]
- **The loans:** he used the oil in his tanks as collateral (security for loans). The receipts were issued by American Express's field-warehousing subsidiary, whose job was to store, inspect and vouch for the oil. [W-Salad] [TIME]
- **The trick:**
  - The tanks were "almost entirely filled with water, with just a little oil on top" [TIFO]. TIME says they were full "with water, topped by a thin slick of oil" and that many were empty.
  - Some tanks had hidden compartments. [W-Salad]
  - Oil was pumped between tanks through "a jungle of pipes", on weekends, so the same oil was counted many times. [TIME] [W-Salad]
- **The inspection:** "De Angelis' men duped Amexco with surprising ease." Often one of them would "clamber to the top of a tank, drop in a weighted tape measure", then "shout down to an Amexco inspector on the ground that the tank was 90% full". So the man who climbed and shouted was TINO'S man, and the inspector stayed on the ground. Never show the inspector climbing or shouting. [TIME, 4 June 1965]
- **Empty tanks:** "Sometimes the tanks were indeed full—with water, topped by a thin slick of oil. Usually many were empty." [TIME]
- **The scale:**
  - Loans of "more than $150 million from 51 different financial institutions" [TIFO]; "$180 million in loans" [W-Salad]. The script says "over 150 million", which fits both.
  - His claimed stock "exceeded the stock available in the entire United States, according to the Department of Agriculture". [W-Tino]
  - About 1.8 billion lbs were expected, and scarcely 100 million lbs were found. [TIME]
- **The collapse:**
  - He tried to corner (buy up nearly all of) the soybean oil market. Prices cracked on 15 November 1963, and Allied filed for bankruptcy on 18-19 November 1963. Creditors then discovered the fraud. [W-Salad] [W-Tino] [TIME]
  - Ira Haupt & Co. (a Wall Street broker) became insolvent. American Express stock dropped "more than a third", and American Express offered $60 million to the claimants. [W-Salad]
- **Buffett:** after the scandal, Warren Buffett bought a large American Express stake (about 5%, in 1964). He reasoned the brand and customers' trust were not broken, and the shares later recovered strongly. [W-Tino] [TIFO] [Fortune 2024]
  - The dollar amounts ($13M vs $20M) and the share of his fund (25% vs 40%) conflict between sources, so they are NOT in the script.
- **Not used:** De Angelis was sentenced in 1965 and released in 1972. The JFK-assassination market panic is deliberately left out.

```
By 1963, a man had borrowed over 150 million dollars... against tanks that were mostly water.

Meet Tino De Angelis, New Jersey's salad oil king.

His giant tanks were full of cooking oil.

Banks lent him money against it. American Express sent inspectors to check.

But Tino's own men would climb up, drop a tape inside, and shout down:

"Ninety percent full!"

And the inspector, on the ground... believed them.

So the banks lent more. And more.

Soon, his papers showed more of that oil than the entire United States had.

But what if someone did look inside?

Then, in November 1963, his big bet on oil prices crashed. His company went bust. Lenders finally checked the tanks.

Inside... was mostly water.

You see, oil floats on water. Ever left salad dressing out? The oil always rises to the top.

Fill a tank with water, add a thin layer of oil, and from the hatch, it looks full.

And the real oil? He pumped it through pipes, tank to tank, so the same oil got counted again and again.

The hardest-working oil in America.

When it came out, American Express shares crashed.

But one young investor bought those shares cheap. He bet people still trusted the brand.

His name? Warren Buffett.

The oil was fake. The trust was real.
```
